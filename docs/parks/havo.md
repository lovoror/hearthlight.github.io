# Hawaiʻi Volcanoes National Park — `HAVO`

> Hawaiʻi · established August 1, 1916 · 1,395.4 km² (344,812 acres) · 1,877,854 recreation visits (2025) · 0–4,169 m ·
> NPS region PWR (today DOI Region 12, Pacific Islands)
>
> Two of the world's most active volcanoes on the south side of the Island of Hawaiʻi: Kīlauea, whose summit caldera
> Kaluapele has been erupting in episodes of lava fountains since December 2024, and the vast, gentle dome of Mauna Loa
> rising to 4,169 m. It is a living, sacred landscape — the home of Pelehonuamea — and in Hearthlight it should feel
> alive: steam from every crack, fresh black lava beside red ʻōhiʻa blossoms, a glow on the clouds at night, and a road
> that runs down a staircase of craters to the sea.

## Reading the map

North up, the main park is a big wedge on the south-east slope of the island. Its north-western part climbs the long, smooth flank of **Mauna Loa** to the summit caldera **Mokuʻāweoweo** (4,169 m) — a huge grey-brown dome crossed by lava flows — and continues down Mauna Loa's south-west rift in a long narrow tongue to the separate-looking **Kahuku Unit**, which reaches the sea west of Nāʻālehu. The south-eastern part is **Kīlauea**, a much lower volcano (1,247 m) that sits on Mauna Loa's shoulder: its summit caldera **Kaluapele** is at the park's north-east edge beside Highway 11 and Volcano Village, its **East Rift Zone** runs 20 km east (the craters of the Chain of Craters, Mauna Ulu, Puʻu ʻŌʻō) and its **Southwest Rift Zone** runs south-west into the **Kaʻū Desert**. South of Kīlauea the land drops in a series of great fault cliffs (the Hilina, Hōlei and Poliokeawe pali) to a roadless lava coast. The ʻŌlaʻa rain forest is a separate green block north of Volcano.

**Highway 11** is the only road through: it comes from Hilo in the north-east, passes Volcano Village and the **park entrance** at the caldera's north-east corner, runs along the north edge of Kaluapele and then south-west across the Kaʻū Desert's edge to Pāhala, Nāʻālehu and the Kahuku Unit. At the summit, **Crater Rim Drive** used to circle the whole caldera; since the 2018 collapse only two pieces remain. **Crater Rim Drive West** runs from the entrance past the Kīlauea Visitor Center (renovation until late 2026; a temporary Welcome Center operates at Kīlauea Military Camp, 2 km in), Volcano House, the Kūkamāhuākea steam vents and Haʻakulamanu sulphur banks to the **Kīlauea Overlook** and **Uēkahuna** bluff above the caldera's west side. **Crater Rim Drive East** runs south-east past the **Kīlauea Iki** overlook, **Nāhuku** lava tube, Puʻupuaʻi and the **Devastation** trail to the start of **Chain of Craters Road**. Between the two, the south half of the caldera and the old road around it are closed.

**Chain of Craters Road** is the park's great drive: 19 mi / 31 km from Crater Rim Drive down to the sea, dropping ~1,130 m past a line of pit craters (Lua Manu, Puhimau, Koʻokoʻolau, Hiʻiaka, Pauahi), the **Mauna Ulu** lava shield of 1969–74 and Puʻuhuluhulu, then over the **Hōlei Pali** in switchbacks (Kealakomo overlook) to the coastal plain, the **Puʻuloa petroglyphs**, and the end of the road where lava has crossed it, next to the **Hōlei Sea Arch**; a gravel emergency route continues east over the lava toward Kalapana. From Chain of Craters Road the narrow **Hilina Pali Road** branches west to Kulanaokuaiki campground and the **Hilina Pali Overlook** on the edge of the cliff. North-west of the summit, **Mauna Loa Road** (one lane, 11.5 mi / 18.5 km) climbs past the Tree Molds and **Kīpukapuaulu** forest island to the **Mauna Loa Lookout** at 2,031 m, where the Mauna Loa Trail begins the long walk to the Red Hill (Puʻuʻulaʻula) cabin and the summit cabin on Mokuʻāweoweo's rim.

The backcountry — the Kaʻū Desert (with the Footprints left in ash in 1790), the East Rift beyond Nāpau, the coast (Kaʻaha, Halapē, Keauhou, ʻĀpua Point, with shelters and rough trails down the pali) and Mauna Loa — is huge and open lava. Everything is organised by the volcanoes: the rift zones are lines of craters and cones, lava flows are labelled with their year on the maps (1959, 1969–74, 1971, 1974, 1982…), and the eruption decides what is open.

### Water

- **No rivers or lakes**: water sinks straight into the porous lava. Rain falls on the windward (north-east) side — the summit gets ~2.5 m a year and the rain forest (ʻŌlaʻa, Kīlauea Iki, Nāhuku) is lush — while the Kaʻū Desert in the rain shadow gets far less and is further dried by acid vog.
- **Steam and gas**: rainwater heated by hot rock rises as steam at Kūkamāhuākea and Wahinekapu; Haʻakulamanu vents sulphur gases; the eruption releases 1,000–5,000 tonnes of SO₂ a day even between episodes, which becomes vog downwind.
- **Lava as water-like terrain**: during eruption episodes lava fountains and flows fill the floor of Halemaʻumaʻu; lava tubes once carried rivers of lava (Nāhuku); old lava lakes (Kīlauea Iki) are still hot underneath.
- **The sea**: a rugged south coast of black lava cliffs and benches pounded by swell, small cove beaches (Halapē, Keauhou, ʻĀpua), the Hōlei Sea Arch, anchialine pools; hawksbill and green turtles; no swimming beaches near the road.
- **Brackish ponds and springs** at a few coastal sites; drinking water only at the summit and must be treated elsewhere.

### Relief

From the sea to 4,169 m at Mauna Loa's summit, but the slopes are gentle: Mauna Loa is a broad shield rising a few degrees, and Kīlauea's summit is only 1,247 m. Relief is concentrated in sudden steps: the **caldera walls** (Kaluapele's cliffs, 100–500 m deep since the 2018 collapse, Halemaʻumaʻu a crater within the caldera), the **pit craters** of the Chain of Craters (vertical-walled holes 50–200 m deep), **cinder and spatter cones** (Puʻupuaʻi, Puʻuhuluhulu, Puʻu ʻŌʻō), low **lava shields** (Mauna Ulu), and the **pali** — the giant fault scarps of the Hilina and Hōlei pali, 300–450 m high, that step the land down to the coast. In the game: the caldera as a deep pit with layered cliff walls and a smoking crater in its floor; pit craters as round cliff-edged holes along the road; the pali as long cliff bands with the road switchbacking down; cones and Mauna Ulu as small 3D hills; Mauna Loa as a vast, low 3D dome filling the north-west horizon; lava fields as flat but rough terrain with ropy (pāhoehoe) and clinkery (ʻaʻā) textures; lava tubes as walk-through caves.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: coastal strand and pili grass on the lava plain (0–300 m) → open lava with pioneer ʻōhiʻa, ʻamaʻu ferns with red new fronds and lichens (300–900 m) → **ʻōhiʻa–hāpuʻu tree-fern rain forest** on the windward summit (Kīlauea Iki, Nāhuku, ʻŌlaʻa, 1,000–1,300 m) and kīpuka of old koa and soapberry (Kīpukapuaulu) → the Kaʻū Desert's bare ash and cinder in the rain shadow → subalpine scrub of pūkiawe, ʻōhelo and ʻaʻaliʻi on Mauna Loa (2,000–3,000 m) → alpine lava desert with almost nothing (3,000–4,169 m).
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#16161A` fresh black pāhoehoe with a blue-silver sheen · `#5E5B5A` weathered grey lava · `#8A4B32` rusty red cinder and ʻaʻā · `#FF5A1F` molten lava and fountains · `#FFB347` incandescent glow · `#E2C542` sulphur yellow · `#B79A74` Kaʻū Desert ash · `#D12A2A` red lehua blossoms · `#4C8A3C` tree-fern and ʻōhiʻa green · `#C8C3B8` vog haze · `#1F6FA6` the Pacific.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): nēnē (Hawaiian goose, the state bird, on roadsides and lawns — endemic and endangered) · ʻapapane and ʻamakihi (red and green honeycreepers in the ʻōhiʻa) · ʻiʻiwi (rare) · ʻōmaʻo (Hawaiian thrush, in the rain forest) · ʻio (Hawaiian hawk) · koaʻe kea (white-tailed tropicbird, gliding and nesting in the caldera walls) · kōlea (Pacific golden plover, winter) · ʻōpeʻapeʻa (Hawaiian hoary bat, the only native land mammal) · honuʻea (hawksbill turtle, nesting on remote beaches) and honu (green turtle) · Kamehameha butterfly (pulelehua) · happy-face spider (rain forest) · feral goats and pigs are fenced out.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): ʻōhiʻa lehua (red « pom-pom » blossoms, the first tree on new lava) · hāpuʻu (tree fern) · ʻamaʻu (lava fern, red new fronds) · ʻōhelo (red berries, sacred to Pele — traditionally offered to her before tasting) · koa (Kīpukapuaulu) · pūkiawe · ʻaʻaliʻi · the rare Kaʻū silversword (ʻāhinahina), replanted on Mauna Loa's upper slopes.
- **Weather & seasons**: little seasonality — the summit is cool, damp and changeable all year (heavier rain November–March), the coast hot and dry. Trade winds push vog to the south-west; « Kona » wind days bring vog over the summit. Mauna Loa's summit freezes at night and can be snowy in winter. The real « season » is the eruption: episodes days to weeks apart.
- **Night**: the eruption glow reflected on clouds and steam, visible from the rim overlooks; lava fountains during episodes; the Milky Way from Mauna Loa Road and the coast; the park's evening « After Dark in the Park » talks.
- **Sounds**: the rumble and roar of fountaining vents, the hiss of steam vents, tephra pattering during episodes, wind across the Kaʻū Desert, surf booming against the lava cliffs, ʻapapane song in the ʻōhiʻa, nēnē honks, rain drumming on tree-fern fronds, and — respectfully — chant (oli) offered at the rim.

## Moments

- **Eruption episodes at Halemaʻumaʻu**: since December 23, 2024 Kīlauea's summit has erupted in 54 fountaining episodes (as of late September 2026, with episode 55 awaited): each lasts a few hours to a day, with lava fountains that at their peak rise hundreds of metres, then the vents pause for days or weeks, glowing and spattering. Viewing is from the open rim (Kīlauea Overlook, Uēkahuna, Keanakākoʻi, Volcano House); night is best.
- **The glow at night**: even between episodes, the vents glow and flicker; steam lit orange over the caldera.
- **Sunrise at Uēkahuna or Kīlauea Iki**, when the steam and the caldera are lit from the east.
- **Nēnē season**: nēnē nest and raise goslings from about October to March (some roads and trails close).
- **Lehua bloom**: ʻōhiʻa flower much of the year, with honeycreepers feeding in the red blossoms.
- **Honuʻea nesting**: hawksbill turtles nest on remote coastal beaches from about May to December (volunteers monitor them).
- **Mauna Loa awake**: its last eruption (from November 27, 2022, for about two weeks) lit up the north-east rift — a rare event on a volcano that erupts every few decades; snow can dust its summit in winter.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a **1280 × 1024-tile** map centred on Kīlauea, ~40× compression overall. Near true scale: the Kīlauea summit set piece (Kaluapele ~4 × 3 km → ~1 km across, with the overlooks, Volcano House, the steam vents, Kīlauea Iki and Nāhuku at walking distances), the Kīlauea Iki crater floor, Nāhuku (a real ~180 m walk-through tube). Compressed ×10: Chain of Craters Road (31 km → ~3 km of winding road with 6–8 pit craters, the pali switchbacks and the sea arch at the bottom). Heavily compressed: the Kaʻū Desert and the coast trails (Halapē as one cove), Mauna Loa as a vast 3D dome filling the north-west with the Mauna Loa Road and trail climbing it. The Kahuku Unit and ʻŌlaʻa can be cut or become a small separate vignette.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: hubs at the Kīlauea summit (visitor centre and hālau, Volcano House, the rim overlooks), Nāhuku/Kīlauea Iki, the end of Chain of Craters Road, Nāmakanipaio campground. 3D landmarks: Kaluapele with Halemaʻumaʻu and its vents and fountains, Puʻupuaʻi, Mauna Ulu, Puʻu ʻŌʻō on the horizon, the Hōlei Sea Arch, the Hilina Pali, Mauna Loa. Interiors: the Kīlauea Visitor Center, Volcano House's lobby and dining room facing the crater, the Volcano Art Center gallery, Nāhuku lava tube, a backcountry cabin on Mauna Loa.
- **Activities**: hiking (above); **photo spots**: the eruption from Kīlauea Overlook and Uēkahuna at night, steam at Wahinekapu, Kīlauea Iki from the rim, the Nāhuku entrance, pit craters along the road, Hōlei Sea Arch, the petroglyphs (photos only), Hilina Pali Overlook, nēnē; **eruption watching** as a live event; **ranger programmes**: rim talks on the eruption and Pele, cultural demonstrations (hula, lei making, lauhala weaving at the Volcano Art Center), « After Dark in the Park »; **Young Ranger** ideas: tell pāhoehoe from ʻaʻā by touch and sound, read lava flow years on the map, count nēnē from a safe distance, learn five Hawaiian place names and their meanings, measure the gas level before a hike; **bike** Crater Rim Drive and the Escape Road; **cave**: Nāhuku; **backpacking** to the coast and up Mauna Loa (permits).
- **Passport stamps**: the official cancellation list (2025) has five: « Hawaii NP », « Jaggar Museum » (a legacy stamp — the museum is gone), « Kīlauea Visitor Center », « Panau Coastal Contact Station » and « Kahuku Unit ». During the renovation they are at the Welcome Center (Kīlauea Military Camp); the Kahuku stamp at the Kahuku contact station; the coastal one at the end-of-road ranger station when staffed.
- **With friends / with the world**: a **night eruption watch** — the open world's great gathering: players line the rim at Kīlauea Overlook and Uēkahuna when an episode starts (a server-wide event with a timer); a **Chain of Craters road trip** from the rain forest to the sea in one ride; a group crossing of the Kīlauea Iki floor; a two-night **Mauna Loa expedition** with the cabins; group photos at the sea arch and at the Nāhuku entrance.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): never cross closure ropes (unstable edges, cracks, gas); never take lava rock, sand, Pele's hair or ʻōhelo berries from the park; never touch or step on petroglyphs; don't disturb offerings (hoʻokupu) left at the rim; give nēnē space and never feed them; drive slowly for nēnē; check air quality (SO₂) and carry water on the coast; clean boots to stop Rapid ʻŌhiʻa Death; respect the sacredness of the place — watch quietly.
- **Implementation challenges**: an **erupting vent** with episodic fountains, glow and lava lakes on a schedule (a new kind of timed event); **lava** as terrain (fresh flows, hot lakes, crusted floors, tubes as caves); **gas and vog** changing with the wind; **deep pit craters and a collapsed caldera** with multi-level cliffs; **steam** from the ground; a **sea arch and surf-battered lava cliffs**; a giant low **shield volcano** as a backdrop; sensitive cultural sites that must be shown respectfully (no looting, no climbing).
