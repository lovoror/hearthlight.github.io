# Haleakalā National Park — `HALE`

> Hawaiʻi (Maui) · established July 1, 1961 (part of Hawaii National Park since August 1, 1916) · 135.5 km² (33,489 acres) ·
> 853,711 recreation visits (2025) · 0–3,055 m · NPS region PWR (today DOI Region 12, Pacific Islands)
>
> East Maui's great volcano, « the house of the sun »: a cold summit above a sea of clouds, a vast red-and-grey cinder
> desert (« the crater ») dotted with cones and silverswords, and — on the other side of the mountain, reached only by the
> winding Hāna Highway — the warm, wet Kīpahulu coast with waterfalls, bamboo and stream pools. In Hearthlight it should
> feel like two parks in one: a hushed, starry, Martian summit you reach before dawn, and a green, dripping valley by the sea.

## Reading the map

North up, the park looks like a long animal lying east–west across the top of East Maui. Its **western half** is the **summit district**: a triangle whose north-west corner is the park entrance on HI 378 (Hosmer Grove campground and the **Park Headquarters Visitor Center** at 2,134 m), and whose south-west corner is the summit. Between them the park road climbs in tight red switchbacks along the western rim — past the Halemauʻu trailhead (2,436 m), **Leleiwi Overlook** and **Kalahaku Overlook** (2,842 m, no left turn when driving up) — to the **Haleakalā Visitor Center** (2,969 m) on the crater rim and the **Puʻuʻulaʻula summit** (3,055 m), the highest point of Maui, with the white domes of the Haleakalā Observatories (« Science City », closed to the public) just beyond.

East of the rim spreads the **crater**, the park's heart: not a true volcanic crater but a huge erosional valley ~11 km long, 3 km wide and ~790 m deep, floored with red, black, yellow and grey cinders and dotted with cinder cones (Kaluʻuoʻōʻō, Puʻu o Māui, Ka Moa o Pele, Halāliʻi, Puʻu Naue, Puʻu Nole, Puʻu Maile, ʻŌʻilipuʻu). It is roadless wilderness crossed by two National Recreation Trails: **Keoneheʻeheʻe (Sliding Sands)** from the visitor centre down the south-west wall, and **Halemauʻu** from the north-west rim down switchbacks cut into the cliff; between them the valley floor holds the Silversword Loop, Kawilinau (a 20 m pit), Pele's Paint Pot and three wilderness cabins — **Hōlua** (north-west, below the Halemauʻu switchbacks), **Kapalaoa** (south side) and **Palikū** (at the east end, where rain forest pours over the rim). The crater opens north through **Koʻolau Gap** toward Keʻanae and south through **Kaupō Gap**, where the unmaintained Kaupō Trail drops out of the park across private land to the coast. The high **Kalapawili Ridge** closes the crater's east end.

The **middle** of the park is the **Kīpahulu Valley Biological Reserve**, a steep, cloud-soaked rain-forest valley running from Kalapawili Ridge down to the sea — closed to all entry to protect some of Hawaiʻi's rarest birds, snails and plants. Its lower end is the **Kīpahulu district** in the south-east corner, on the coast beside the Hāna Highway: the **Kīpahulu Visitor Center**, a drive-up campground on the grassy cliffs above Kukui Bay, the Kūloa Point loop to the mouth of **ʻOheʻo Gulch** with its tiered stream **pools** (closed to swimming), and the **Pīpīwai Trail** climbing 3 km up the gulch past Makahiku Falls, a giant banyan and a bamboo forest to **Waimoku Falls**. The Hāna Highway winds in from Hāna in the north-east and continues south-west as a rough road past Kaupō.

The two districts are **not linked by any road inside the park**: going from the summit to Kīpahulu means driving back down to Kahului and round the island, 3 hours or more. Relief organises everything: the mountain rises in one long ramp from the sea at Kīpahulu to the 3,055 m summit, and the crater is a sunken bowl near its top; clouds usually pile up at 1,500–2,500 m (the trade-wind inversion), so the summit often sits above a sea of clouds while Kīpahulu stands in the rain beneath it.

### Water

- **Summit and crater**: no permanent streams or lakes; rain and fog soak into the cinders; springs and seeps only near Palikū, where the rain forest of the east rim drips into the crater. Fog and clouds are the water story up here — the trade-wind inversion keeps a cloud sea at 1,500–2,500 m.
- **Kīpahulu**: one of the wettest places on Maui (several metres of rain a year) — **ʻOheʻo Gulch** and the **Pīpīwai Stream** tumble through a chain of plunge pools to the sea at Kūloa Point; **Waimoku Falls** (~120 m) and **Makahiku Falls** (~56 m) pour off sheer walls; flash floods can raise the stream several metres in minutes, which is why the pools are closed to swimming. The streams hold native ʻoʻopu (gobies that climb waterfalls with suction fins) and ʻōpae (freshwater shrimp).
- **The sea**: a rocky, surf-battered coast at Kīpahulu (no swimming beaches); Kukui Bay below the campground.
- Nicknamed « Seven Sacred Pools » by tourism, the pools are more than seven and the name is not traditional — the park calls them the Pools of ʻOheʻo.

### Relief

From sea level at Kīpahulu to 3,055 m at Puʻuʻulaʻula, a shield volcano whose east side has been carved by erosion: the summit ridge forms the western rim of the crater, which sinks ~790 m to a floor at ~2,000–2,300 m, with 150–200 m high cinder cones on it; the crater walls are steep cliffs (the Halemauʻu switchbacks cross one), and two great gaps (Koʻolau, Kaupō) breach the rim. Below the east rim the land plunges into the deep Kīpahulu Valley, then drops to sea cliffs. In the game: the summit as a high rim plateau with a sheer inner wall and switchback stairs down; the crater floor as a wide flat basin with 3D cinder cones in several colours and lava flows as darker streaks; the gaps as broad ramps out of the bowl; the observatory domes and the summit shelter as landmarks on the highest point; Kīpahulu as a narrow gulch with stepped waterfalls and pools descending to sea cliffs. A cloud-sea layer that the player climbs through is the single most important effect.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: Kīpahulu's lowland — kukui, hala, mango, guava, banyan, non-native bamboo, taro loʻi (0–600 m) → the closed Kīpahulu Valley's native ʻōhiʻa–koa rain forest with tree ferns (600–2,400 m) → subalpine shrubland of pūkiawe, ʻōhelo, naʻenaʻe, māmane and sandalwood around the park entrance and HQ (1,900–2,500 m), with the planted conifers of Hosmer Grove → alpine cinder desert with scattered **ʻāhinahina (silverswords)** and bunch grasses (2,500–3,055 m) → Palikū's misty meadow and forest at the crater's wet east end.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#9C3B2A` rust-red cinders · `#3B3534` black cinder and lava · `#C9A23F` sulphur-yellow and ochre cones (Pele's Paint Pot) · `#8E8A86` grey ash · `#C9D4D9` silver silversword rosettes · `#F4F1EA` the cloud sea at dawn · `#FF9A3C` sunrise gold · `#2F6B3A` Kīpahulu green · `#6FA55A` bamboo · `#3E8E9E` stream-pool water · `#1B5E8C` the sea off Kīpahulu.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): nēnē (Hawaiian goose — reintroduced to the crater in 1946 by Boy Scouts carrying goslings in their packs; on roads, in the crater and at Palikū) · ʻuaʻu (Hawaiian petrel, endangered seabird nesting in burrows around the summit; flies in at dusk) · ʻiʻiwi, ʻapapane and ʻamakihi (Hosmer Grove) · kiwikiu (Maui parrotbill, critically endangered, in the closed rain forest) · ʻākohekohe (crested honeycreeper) · pueo (Hawaiian short-eared owl, hunting the grasslands by day) · ʻōpeʻapeʻa (Hawaiian hoary bat) · ʻoʻopu ʻalamoʻo (waterfall-climbing goby) and ʻōpae (stream shrimp) at Kīpahulu · endemic wolf spiders and insects living on the bare cinders · honu (green turtles) off the coast.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): ʻāhinahina (Haleakalā silversword — grows for decades as a silver rosette, flowers once in a tall purple stalk, usually June–October, then dies) · naʻenaʻe (Haleakalā greensword relatives) · pūkiawe (red and white berries) · ʻōhelo (sacred berries) · māmane (yellow flowers, nēnē food) · Haleakalā sandalwood (ʻiliahi) · kalo (taro) and the bamboo forest at Kīpahulu.
- **Weather & seasons**: the summit is cold and changeable all year — frost and occasional snow in winter, strong UV, wind, clouds racing up through the gaps in the afternoon; Kīpahulu is warm and wet, with the heaviest rains in winter and flash floods any time. The cloud sea usually sits below the summit in the morning.
- **Night**: one of the darkest, clearest skies on Earth (hence the observatories): the Milky Way over the crater, the summit open for stargazing after sunset (no reservation needed), ʻuaʻu calls in the dark; the glow of nothing at all; at Kīpahulu the sound of the stream and surf.
- **Sounds**: wind whistling over cinders and total silence in the crater (the park protects its natural quiet), crunching of cinders underfoot, nēnē honks, the ʻuaʻu's eerie night call, honeycreepers at Hosmer Grove, the hollow knocking of bamboo in the wind, waterfalls and surf at Kīpahulu, oli (chant) at sunrise.

## Moments

- **Sunrise above the clouds**: the park's signature — visitors reach the rim or summit in the dark (reservation for 3–7 am), and the sun rises over a sea of clouds, lighting the crater's colours; a Hawaiian chant sometimes greets it. Sunset and stargazing need no reservation.
- **Silversword bloom**: individual ʻāhinahina send up tall flower stalks mostly June–October; a « good year » has hundreds in bloom around Kalahaku and the Silversword Loop.
- **Brocken spectre at Leleiwi**: with the sun low behind you and clouds in the crater, your shadow appears ringed with a rainbow.
- **ʻUaʻu return at dusk**: Hawaiian petrels return to their summit burrows after dark in the nesting season (roughly spring–autumn).
- **Nēnē with goslings** in the crater and around HQ (nesting in the wetter months).
- **Kīpahulu after rain**: Waimoku and the ʻOheʻo falls at full roar (and the pools closing for flash floods).
- **Snow on the summit** on rare winter mornings.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a **1024 × 768-tile** map for a 23 × 16 km park (~15–20× compression), keeping the long east–west shape: the summit district and crater fill the west two-thirds, the closed Kīpahulu Valley is a steep green no-go slope in the middle, and Kīpahulu is a small coastal pocket in the south-east reached by its own road in from the east edge (the Hāna Highway as an arrival « from off-map », since the real drive around the island is 3 hours). Near true scale: the summit (VC, Pā Kaʻoao, the summit shelter, the observatory domes), the rim overlooks, the first kilometre of Sliding Sands, Kīpahulu's VC, pools and Pīpīwai Trail (6.4 km → ~2 km). Compressed ×5–10: the crater (11 km → ~1.2 km, keeping 8–10 cones and the three cabins), the park road switchbacks (16.5 km → ~2 km of hairpins).
- **Hubs & set pieces**: hubs at the Haleakalā VC & summit (the sunrise gathering), Park HQ & Hosmer Grove, the crater cabins, Kīpahulu VC & campground. 3D landmarks: the crater rim and its cones, the summit shelter and observatory domes, Kalahaku's silverswords, the Halemauʻu switchbacks and Rainbow Bridge, Hanakauhi, Waimoku Falls, the banyan and bamboo forest, the ʻOheʻo pools. Interiors: the Haleakalā VC, the Park HQ VC, a wilderness cabin (bunks, wood stove), the Kīpahulu VC, a thatched hale in the cultural demonstration area.
- **Activities**: hiking (above); **photo spots**: sunrise over the clouds from Puʻuʻulaʻula and the VC, Kalahaku's silverswords, Leleiwi's Brocken spectre, Pele's Paint Pot, the Halemauʻu switchbacks, Waimoku Falls, the bamboo forest, the ʻOheʻo pools from Kūloa Point; **stargazing** at the summit; **wildlife**: nēnē, ʻuaʻu at dusk, honeycreepers at Hosmer Grove; **ranger programmes**: sunrise and summit talks, silversword walks, cultural demonstrations at Kīpahulu (kapa, poi, lauhala); **Young Ranger** ideas: count silversword rosettes without stepping on their roots, learn the story of Māui slowing the sun, match cinder colours to their minerals, identify three honeycreepers by song, help check nēnē from a distance; **horse** riding into the crater (horses and mules are allowed on the Sliding Sands, Halemauʻu and Supply trails); **camping** in the crater cabins; **bike** tours down the park road (commercial tours start outside the park); no swimming at ʻOheʻo.
- **Passport stamps**: the official cancellation list (2025) has six for Haleakalā: « Kipahulu », « Kīpahulu Visitor Center », « Headquarters Visitor Center », « Haleakalā Summit », « Makawao » and « Maui, Hawaii ». They are kept at the Park HQ and Haleakalā visitor centres (summit district) and the Kīpahulu Visitor Center; the Makawao and « Maui, Hawaii » stamps belong to the park's partner outlets.
- **With friends / with the world**: the **sunrise gathering** is the open world's natural event — everyone on the rim at the same in-game dawn, a shared cloud sea, a chant, a mass photo; a **crater traverse** party (one car left at Halemauʻu, the group descending Sliding Sands together and meeting at a cabin for the night); a **Pīpīwai** walk ending under Waimoku; a Brocken-spectre hunt at Leleiwi. Group photos: at the summit sign, in the bamboo forest, around a blooming silversword (from the trail).
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): stay on trails (the cinder crust and silversword roots are fragile); never touch or photograph yourself hugging silverswords, never take cinders or rocks; don't feed nēnē (they get hit by cars); drive slowly for nēnē and ʻuaʻu at night; don't enter the Kīpahulu Valley reserve or closed pools; no stacking of rocks or building of shelters; respect Haleakalā as a sacred place (wao akua); dim lights at night for the stars and the petrels.
- **Implementation challenges**: the **cloud sea** below the summit (a volumetric layer the player climbs through), **sunrise lighting** as a timed event with a reservation-style limit, a **high-altitude cinder desert** with multicoloured cones, **two districts with no connection** (a map with an off-map drive or a fast-travel link), steep **waterfalls and stepped pools** with flash floods, a closed reserve that must look inviting yet be off-limits, very dark skies.
