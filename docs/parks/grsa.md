# Great Sand Dunes National Park and Preserve — `GRSA`

> Colorado · established September 24, 2004 · 434.4 km² (107,345.73 acres; the park, with the preserve ~600 km²) · 432,498 recreation visits (2025) ·
> ~2,290–4,146 m · NPS region IMR
>
> The tallest dunes in North America, piled against the Sangre de Cristo Mountains at the edge of the high, flat San Luis Valley, with a snowmelt creek that runs across the sand in waves in spring, forests and alpine lakes above, and some of the darkest skies in the country. In Hearthlight it should feel like a beach at 2,500 m: a creek to splash in, a sea of sand to climb and slide down, the mountains close behind, and a night sky full of stars.

## Reading the map

North up, the brochure is dominated by two shapes. On the **west**, the flat floor of the **San Luis Valley** (a high desert basin at ~2,300 m) fills the left half: its **sand sheet** (grassland on sandy soil) and, in the south-west corner, the **sabkha** (salty wetlands with small lakes: Twin, Cotton, Dollar, and the state wildlife area's San Luis Lake just outside). The park boundary is a big right-angled block here: a straight west edge along the Baca National Wildlife Refuge, a straight south edge along County Lane 6 North. On the **east**, the **Sangre de Cristo Mountains** rise steeply from north-west to south-east as a long wall of peaks over 4,000 m. Their west slopes and crest, from Marble Mountain and Milwaukee Peak in the north down past Mount Herard and Mount Zwischen to Mosca Pass, form the **national preserve**, whose boundary follows the crest and then the ridges down to the east.

The **dunefield** lies where the two meet: an oval of pale sand ~11 km long, tucked into the curve of the mountains in the east-centre of the map. Its most complex dunes — the **Star Dune Complex** — fill its north-western part; **Hidden Dune** (~225 m high, the tallest on the 2023 map) sits in the middle, **Star Dune** (~224 m) on the southern edge about 2 km west of **High Dune on First Ridge** (~210 m), the nearest big dune to the visitor area. Two creeks come down from the mountains and wrap round the sand. **Medano Creek** runs along the dunes' east and south-east edge — the beach of the main visitor area. **Sand Creek** runs along the north and west edge. Both sink into the valley floor.

The **road network** is tiny. CO 150 comes up from the south-west across the sand sheet, passes the Oasis store and campground just outside, enters the park at the entrance station, and passes park headquarters and the **visitor centre** (2,492 m). It ends at **Dunes Parking** by Medano Creek, with a spur north to the **Piñon Flats** campground and amphitheatre. Beyond Piñon Flats the **Medano Pass Primitive Road** heads north along the dunes' eastern edge: first a sandy stretch to **Point of No Return** (the end for ordinary cars), then 4WD only past the **Sand Pit** and **Castle Creek** picnic areas and the Sand Ramp trailhead. From there it climbs Medano Creek's canyon through the preserve (nine creek crossings, 21 primitive campsites) to **Medano Pass** (3,043 m) on the preserve's north-east edge, and down to CO 69 in the Wet Mountain Valley (35 km in all). Trails: the Mosca Pass Trail east from the Montville trailhead up to Mosca Pass on the crest; the Sand Ramp Trail round the north-east edge of the dunes to Sand Creek; the Medano Lake trail below Mount Herard; the Sand Creek lakes trails in the north, reached from Music Pass on the east side; Liberty Gate on the north-west corner.

**Relief** is simple and dramatic: the valley floor at 2,290–2,450 m, the dunes rising up to ~225 m above their base (their crests reach ~2,700 m near the mountains), the foothills at 2,500–2,700 m, and the Sangre de Cristo crest at 3,600–4,146 m (Tijeras Peak, the preserve's highest), only 8–15 km from the dunes.

### Water

Everything here is shaped by two snowmelt creeks. **Medano Creek** rises near Medano Pass, runs down its canyon through the preserve, reaches the sand ~8 km upstream of the visitor area, then spreads into a wide, shallow, braided sheet along the dunes' east and south-east edge. It sinks into the valley floor south-west of the main beach. In an average year it arrives at Dunes Parking in April, peaks in late May–early June (~40 cubic feet per second where it meets the sand) and dries at the main area by July, while still trickling near Castle Creek into the autumn. Its famous **surge flow** comes from underwater sand ridges (antidunes) that build up and collapse, sending small waves down the creek every 20 seconds or so — enough, in wet years, for children to float on inflatables. In 2026 the snowpack was near a record low and the creek stayed dry at the main parking. **Sand Creek** does the same along the dunes' north and west sides, from the Sand Creek lakes. Both creeks carry sand back round to the upwind side, part of what keeps the dunefield in place. **Mosca Creek** runs past Montville. West of the dunes, springs rise on the **sand sheet** (Big Spring, Little Spring, Indian Spring creeks) and the ground turns into the **sabkha**: seasonal salty lakes and wetlands (Twin, Cotton, Dollar lakes) with shorebirds, next to the state's San Luis Lakes. Up high, cirque lakes: Upper and Lower Sand Creek lakes, Little Sand Creek Lakes, Medano Lake.

### Relief

From ~2,290 m on the sabkha to 4,146 m on Tijeras Peak. The valley floor is almost perfectly flat; the **dunes** rise from it in ridges up to ~225 m high (Hidden Dune, Star Dune), shaped by winds that blow from the south-west most of the year and from the north-east in storms, which piles them into tall **star dunes** and **reversing dunes** against the curve of the mountains. The **foothills** (Piñon Flats, Montville, 2,500–2,700 m) are a bench of alluvial fans. The **Sangre de Cristo** rise abruptly behind them, a fault-block range with a crest of 3,600–4,150 m, cut by glacial valleys with cirque lakes at their heads.

In game terms: the valley at level 0, the creek beach at level 0–1, the dunes as a field of smooth sand heaps rising to level 4–5 (not cliffs: walkable, steep slopes where you slide back down), the foothills at 1–2 with banks, the range from 3 to 7 with 3D peaks (Mount Herard, Tijeras, Milwaukee, Mount Zwischen, Mosca Pass as a saddle). The dunes need their own terrain: slip faces, ripples, crests, bowls.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: valley floor (2,290–2,450 m): rabbitbrush, greasewood and saltgrass on the sabkha, Indian ricegrass, blowout grass and scurfpea on the sand sheet and dune edges, prairie sunflowers on the sand in late summer; creeks: narrowleaf cottonwood and willow; foothills (2,450–2,800 m): piñon pine and juniper, ponderosa pine, then aspen and Douglas-fir; subalpine (2,800–3,500 m): Engelmann spruce, subalpine fir, limber and Rocky Mountain bristlecone pines; alpine (above ~3,500 m): tundra, talus and snowfields.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#E9D9B8` dune sand, pale gold in sun · `#C7A77A` dune shadow and wet sand · `#8E7A63` dark magnetite ripples on the sand · `#B9B08F` sand-sheet grass · `#E7E3D6` sabkha salt crust · `#6E8C6B` piñon-juniper green · `#2F4A38` spruce-fir · `#E5B83C` aspen and cottonwood gold · `#7FA9B8` shallow creek on sand · `#5C6A82` blue-grey Sangre de Cristo rock.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): elk (sand-sheet grasslands and the preserve), mule deer (Piñon Flats, often in camp), pronghorn (the sand sheet), black bear (foothills and preserve), bighorn sheep (the high peaks), coyote, Ord's kangaroo rat (the dunes at night — its tracks at dawn), the Great Sand Dunes tiger beetle (found nowhere else, on warm sand in spring and autumn), the giant sand treader camel cricket (another dune endemic), burrowing owl and horned lark (the valley), American avocet and Wilson's phalarope (the sabkha lakes), sandhill cranes (spring and autumn migration over the valley), great horned owl, Rio Grande cutthroat trout (Medano Creek, restored).
- **Flora** (the herbarium): prairie sunflower (July–September, the dunes' flower), blowout grass (a sand-binding grass), Indian ricegrass, scurfpea, rubber rabbitbrush (gold in September), piñon pine (autumn nuts), narrowleaf cottonwood, Rocky Mountain bee plant, aspen, Rocky Mountain bristlecone pine (on the high slopes).
- **Weather & seasons**: spring (March–May): the windy season, sand blowing off the crests, snow still possible, the creek arriving; late May–June: creek season and crowds, mosquitoes from mid-June; summer (July–August): hot sand at midday, afternoon thunderstorms with lightning (off the dunes!), cool nights; autumn (September–October): calm, clear, golden cottonwoods and aspens, elk bugling in the preserve; winter (November–February): cold, snow patterns on the dunes, empty and silent.
- **Night**: an International Dark Sky Park: the Milky Way arching over the dunes, full-moon walks on the sand (bright enough to hike without a light), kangaroo rats and beetles out on the sand, owls in the piñons, stargazing programmes at the Piñon Flats amphitheatre.
- **Sounds**: wind hissing sand across the crests, the rare « booming » of an avalanching slip face, the creek's small waves and children shouting, the crunch of sand underfoot, sandhill cranes' calls overhead in March and October, coyotes at night, elk bugles in the autumn preserve.

## Moments

- **Medano Creek's surge flow**: late May to early June in an average year (peak ~40 cfs where the creek meets the sand), waves rolling down the creek every 20 seconds or so; dry at the main beach by July; in 2026 a near-record-low snowpack kept it from reaching the main parking at all.
- **Dune light**: the dunes are best at sunrise and sunset, when low sun throws the ridges into shadow — and in summer those are also the only safe hours for climbing (midday sand can reach ~70 °C / 160 °F).
- **Dark-sky nights**: moonless nights for the Milky Way; full-moon nights for walking the dunes by moonlight.
- **Sandhill cranes**: March and October, thousands fly over the San Luis Valley on migration (the Monte Vista refuge nearby).
- **Spring winds**: March–May, sand streams off the crests in « flags »; the dunes shift.
- **Summer storms**: July–August afternoons, lightning over the dunes: get off the high ridges.
- **Autumn gold**: early to mid-October, cottonwoods along Medano Creek and aspens in the preserve.
- **Snow on the dunes**: winter, white stripes on the ridges and a frozen creek.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: 768 × 768 tiles (24 × 24 chunks), north up, centred on the dunefield and the visitor area, with the Sangre de Cristo as the map's north-east third. Hotspots near true scale at 1 tile ≈ 3 m: the visitor area, Dunes Parking and the Medano Creek beach (~250 × 150 tiles), Piñon Flats. The dunefield is compressed ~4× (11 × 8 km → ~450 × 350 tiles), but its heights are kept as much as the levels allow — the climb up High Dune must feel big. The Medano Pass road is compressed ~10× (~250 tiles of sand and creek crossings to a pass on the map's north-east edge). The preserve's alpine corner (Medano Lake, Mount Herard, the Sand Creek lakes) is compressed ~20× into the top of the map. The valley's sand sheet and sabkha form a flat, open western strip (~150 tiles) with a spring and a salt lake. Cut: the Baca refuge and Liberty Gate (edge), the outside roads.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: the visitor centre and Dunes Parking (the hub), the Medano Creek beach (the open-world plaza), Piñon Flats (camp and amphitheatre), Point of No Return, Castle Creek, Medano Pass. Landmarks: High Dune and Star Dune as the two great sand peaks, the Sangre de Cristo wall behind with Mount Herard and Tijeras Peak as 3D peaks, the dunes' slip faces and crests, the braided creek, Zapata Falls' slot just outside. Interiors: the visitor centre (a dune-science exhibit, a sandboard rack), the Oasis store just outside (the rental shop).
- **Activities**: climbing dunes (no trails: pick a ridge), **sandboarding and sand-sledding** down the slip faces (boards rented outside the park, at the Oasis; the steep faces of High Dune are the runs), wading and floating on Medano Creek in season, building sand castles; photo spots — the dunes with the Sangre de Cristo behind from the visitor centre, High Dune's summit, ripples at sunset, the creek's waves, the Milky Way over the dunes; wildlife spotting (tiger beetles on the sand, kangaroo-rat tracks at dawn, elk on the grassland, cranes overhead); ranger programmes (dune walks, creek science, night-sky talks at the amphitheatre, full-moon walks); Young Ranger ideas: find a tiger beetle, measure how hot the sand is at noon versus the evening, follow an animal's tracks at dawn, count the waves in a surge; vehicles: car to the visitor area, a 4WD jeep ride up the Medano Pass road (a vehicle only this park gets: a sandy track and creek crossings), horses on the Sand Ramp Trail; camping at Piñon Flats and on the primitive road; the mountains for hikers (Medano Lake, Mount Herard).
- **Passport stamps**: the Great Sand Dunes Visitor Center (the park's stamp; to confirm on the Passport stamp finder).
- **With friends / with the world**: the creek beach is the natural gathering place (splashing, castle-building, sliding contests on the first ridge); High Dune's summit is where strangers meet and take the group photo. Party ideas: a sled race down a slip face; a relay to Star Dune before the midday heat; a sand-castle build that the surge waves slowly wash away; a moonlit dune walk together; a 4WD convoy up Medano Pass where passengers spot and the driver avoids the soft sand.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): hike the dunes early or late in summer (hot sand burns: shoes on); get off the dunes when thunderstorms come; bring water — none on the dunes; stay off the creek's banks where plants grow; no vehicles on the dunes or off the road; campfires only in rings; keep 25 yd / 23 m from elk, deer and pronghorn, 100 yd / 91 m from bears; store food (bears visit Piñon Flats); dark-sky rules: no bright lights at night.
- **Implementation challenges**: dune terrain — smooth, walkable sand heaps with slip faces, ripples and crests that are not cliffs, and a sliding mechanic (sandboard, sled, the player sliding back down a steep face); wind-blown sand particles; a creek that is a seasonal state (arriving, peaking with surge waves, drying back upstream) on a sandy, braided bed; the heat of the sand as a timer; a 4WD vehicle with soft-sand physics and creek crossings; the jump from a sea of sand to 4,000 m peaks in a few kilometres; a night sky that must look darker and fuller here than anywhere (and moonlight bright enough to walk by).
