# Mesa Verde National Park — `MEVE`

> Colorado · established June 29, 1906 · 212.4 km² (52,485.17 acres) · 463,130 recreation visits (2025) ·
> ~1,880–2,613 m · NPS region IMR
>
> The ancestral home of the Pueblo peoples: a high, green, canyon-cut tableland where families farmed the mesa tops for
> some 700 years and, in the 1200s, built villages of stone into the alcoves of the canyon walls; today it protects the
> heritage of 27 Pueblos and Tribes and is a World Heritage Site. In the game it should feel like a quiet, respectful
> visit to a living people's old home — long mesa-top drives through pinyon and oak, then the hush of a sunlit alcove
> full of rooms, reached by ladders beside a ranger.

## Reading the map

Mesa Verde is a **tilted tableland**: its high edge is the **North Rim**, a line of cliffs and slopes along the park's north side (~2,450–2,610 m) looking down on the Montezuma and Mancos valleys, and from there the whole surface slopes gently down to the south (~2,050 m at the southern boundary). Canyons cut this slope into long parallel **finger mesas** running north–south — from west to east Wetherill Mesa, Long Mesa, Chapin Mesa, Park Mesa, Moccasin Mesa, Whites Mesa and Big Mesa — separated by Rock, Long, Navajo, Spruce, Cliff, Soda, Moccasin and Morefield canyons. The mesas continue south into the Ute Mountain Ute Reservation (Ute Mountain Tribal Park), which borders the park on the south and west. The Mancos River runs in its canyon along the south-east edge.

The **entrance** is at the top right, where US-160 crosses the park's northern tip between Cortez and Mancos; the Visitor and Research Center stands right by the highway. The park road climbs south in switchbacks past the entrance station and Point Lookout (a butte) to **Morefield Campground** (4 miles) in a broad valley, then swings through a short tunnel and climbs west to the Montezuma Valley Overlook and along the **North Rim**, past the Knife Edge, **Park Point** (the high point, with a fire lookout) and the Geologic Overlook, to the **Far View** area (15 miles: lodge, café, mesa-top village sites). There it forks.

The main road turns south down the spine of **Chapin Mesa** — past the Far View Sites and Cedar Tree Tower — to the all-way stop at the **Mesa Verde Museum** and park headquarters (20–21 miles from the entrance), where Spruce Tree House fills an alcove just east of the museum. South of the museum two 6-mile loops cover the end of the mesa: the **Mesa Top Loop** (west; pithouses, Square Tower House seen across Navajo Canyon, Sun Point View, Sun Temple, with Cliff Palace seen across Cliff Canyon) and the one-way **Cliff Palace Loop** (east; Cliff Palace and Balcony House tour stops, Soda Canyon Overlook trail; closed in winter). The other fork, the seasonal **Wetherill Mesa Road** (12 miles, May–October), leaves Far View westward, crosses the burned North Rim country past the Fire Recovery viewpoint, and runs south down **Wetherill Mesa** to a parking area and kiosk; beyond it no cars go — the paved **Long House Loop** (6 miles) is walked or biked to Step House, Long House, the Badger House Community and Kodak House Overlook. Everything east of Chapin Mesa (Park, Moccasin, Whites and Big mesas, the East Rim) is roadless and closed to visitors.

### Water

Water is the park's hidden story. There are no lakes and no rivers on the mesas; the **Mancos River** flows south-west in its canyon along the park's south-east edge, below the roadless eastern mesas. The mesas are capped by a porous sandstone (the Cliff House Sandstone) lying on an impermeable shale: rain and snowmelt soak down through the rock, stop on the shale and seep out at the back of the canyon alcoves — the reason the alcoves formed, and the reason people built in them (Long House has a spring at its back; Spruce Tree House and others sit at seep lines). Ancestral Pueblo farmers caught runoff with **check dams and terraces** in the draws (the Farming Terrace Trail) and built reservoirs such as **Far View Reservoir** (once called Mummy Lake). Summer monsoon storms fill the canyon bottoms briefly; winter snow on the mesas is the main water source. Seeps and springs feed small hanging gardens and Douglas-fir groves at canyon heads.

### Relief

The park runs from ~1,880 m in the Mancos River canyon on its east edge to 2,613 m at Park Point. It is a **cuesta**: a steep northern escarpment (the North Rim, 300–600 m above the Montezuma and Mancos valleys, with Point Lookout as its prow), then a long surface tilting gently (~2°) to the south, from ~2,450 m at Far View to ~2,050 m at the southern boundary. The surface is split by parallel canyons 100–250 m deep with sheer upper walls of cream-to-tan Cliff House Sandstone, in which wind and seeping water have hollowed **alcoves** — shallow caves 20–90 m wide under overhanging roofs, where the cliff dwellings stand. Below the sandstone, softer grey shale slopes (Menefee) reach the canyon floors.

In the game: the mesa tops as gently stepped plateaus (one level every few hundred metres southward), canyons as 100-tile-long troughs with a painted cream sandstone band on top and grey slopes below; alcoves as overhangs — the cliff dwellings must be 3D models set into the canyon wall (they can't be built from height tiles), reached by stairs, ladders and ledges; the North Rim as a big escarpment with switchbacks and a road tunnel; Park Point with its lookout.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: North Rim and Park Point (2,400–2,610 m) — dense Gambel oak, Utah serviceberry, mountain mahogany, snowberry, with Douglas-fir in shady draws. Mesa tops (2,050–2,450 m) — pinyon pine and Utah juniper woodland, some trees many centuries old, with big sagebrush, yucca, bitterbrush and grasses; large areas burned in 1996–2003 now regrowing as oak brush, grass and standing grey snags. Canyon heads and alcoves — Douglas-fir (the "spruce" of Spruce Tree House), boxelder, seep plants and ferns.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#E6D6B4` Cliff House Sandstone cliffs and alcove walls · `#C9A77C` warm tan masonry of the cliff dwellings · `#8E8A84` grey Menefee shale slopes · `#B55E3C` red-brown soil of the mesa tops · `#2E2520` alcove shadows and desert varnish · `#56704A` pinyon–juniper green · `#8FA05B` Gambel oak (`#D9772E` orange in October) · `#A7A89A` burned snags · `#8A7A5C` Mancos River · `#EEF1F2` winter snow.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): mule deer everywhere along the roads; elk on the North Rim and around Morefield; wild turkeys (once kept by the Ancestral Pueblo people for feathers); black bear in the oak brush (late summer, eating acorns and berries); mountain lion, bobcat, coyote and gray fox (rarely seen); rock squirrels and least chipmunks at overlooks; Steller's and pinyon jays; golden eagles and peregrine falcons on the cliffs; ravens; white-throated swifts and violet-green swallows at the alcoves; canyon wren; plateau lizards on warm stone; bats emerging at dusk.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): pinyon pine (nuts in fall); Utah juniper; Gambel oak (acorns, red-orange in fall); yucca (fibre for sandals, rope and baskets); Utah serviceberry (white blossoms in May); cliffrose; Indian paintbrush and penstemons (May–July); sego lily; globemallow; Douglas-fir in the alcoves.
- **Weather & seasons**: spring — wind, late snow, the tours begin in early May; summer — warm days, cool nights, July–August thunderstorms (don't stand at overlooks in lightning), wildfire smoke some years; fall — clear, crisp, oak brush orange and red; winter — snow on the mesas and the dwellings (seen from overlooks), icy roads, cross-country ski and snowshoe trails, most services closed.
- **Night**: the 100th International Dark Sky Park (2021): very dark skies over the mesas, ranger stargazing and summer evening programmes at the Morefield amphitheatre; bats, owls; the silhouettes of Sleeping Ute Mountain and the La Plata Mountains.
- **Sounds**: wind through pinyon and oak; the canyon wren's song echoing in an alcove; ravens; swifts whistling past the cliff face; boots on wooden ladders and stone steps; a ranger's voice in a quiet alcove; thunder rolling over the mesas; elk bugling in the fall near Morefield.

## Moments

- **Morning light in the alcoves** — the canyons face every direction, but Cliff Palace, facing west, glows gold in the late afternoon, while east-facing Balcony House is lit in the morning.
- **The tour season** — early May to late October: ranger-led tours of Cliff Palace, Balcony House and Long House (tickets open 14 days ahead and go fast); Step House is open without a ticket when Wetherill Mesa is open (late May–October).
- **Oak turning orange** — late September–October on the North Rim and mesa tops.
- **Snow on the dwellings** — winter: Spruce Tree House and Cliff Palace under snow, seen from overlooks.
- **Summer storms** — July–August afternoons: thunderheads over the Four Corners, lightning, then steaming stone.
- **Elk rut** — September–October, bugling near Morefield and the North Rim.
- **Dark-sky nights** — Milky Way over the mesas; ranger astronomy programmes in summer.
- **Sunset from Park Point or the Knife Edge** — over Montezuma Valley toward Sleeping Ute Mountain and Shiprock.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a 768 × 768-tile map, north up. The entrance and North Rim (the 15 miles of road to Far View) shrink to ~350 tiles of winding road (~1:30), keeping the switchbacks, the tunnel and the overlooks. The south end of Chapin Mesa (~3 × 3 km: museum, both loops, Cliff Palace, Balcony House, Sun Temple) is the heart, near true scale at ~1:4 (~350 × 350 tiles), with the canyons between the finger mesas kept deep and narrow; the cliff dwellings themselves are at true scale (Cliff Palace ~90 m long → ~60 tiles). Wetherill Mesa's end (~2 × 2 km) sits south-west at the same scale; the roadless eastern mesas are a compressed, visible-but-closed band.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: the Visitor and Research Center (interior: exhibits, the passport stamp); Morefield Campground and village; Far View Lodge and Terrace; the **Mesa Verde Museum** (interior: dioramas of the eras, pottery, the park film); the Wetherill Mesa kiosk. 3D set pieces: **Cliff Palace**, **Balcony House**, **Spruce Tree House**, **Long House**, **Step House**, Square Tower House, Sun Temple, Cedar Tree Tower, the Far View pueblos and reservoir, pithouses under shelters, the Park Point fire lookout; Sleeping Ute Mountain, Shiprock and the La Plata Mountains on the horizon.
- **Activities**: **ranger-led cliff-dwelling tours** as the signature experience — a tour ticket (a timed slot you get ahead of time), a ranger who leads the group down steps and up ladders, stories told at stops, the Balcony House tunnel and cliff climb as a gentle physical challenge; self-guided Step House; the Mesa Top Loop's 11 stops as a timeline of 700 years (pithouse → pueblo → cliff dwelling), with a **Pueblo-perspective audio tour** as the model for in-game narration; overlooks (a "spot the dwellings" game from Sun Point View, where a dozen are visible); hiking (table), biking the Long House Loop; Junior Ranger booklet; evening programmes and stargazing at Morefield; winter snowshoeing and cross-country skiing on the Cliff Palace Loop. Photo spots: Cliff Palace from its overlook, Spruce Tree House from the museum path, Square Tower House, Sun Point View, Balcony House from Soda Canyon Overlook, Cedar Tree Tower, Park Point, the Knife Edge at sunset.
- **Passport stamps**: Mesa Verde Visitor and Research Center; Mesa Verde Museum on Chapin Mesa.
- **With friends / with the world**: a tour group is a natural party — players gather at the tour stop at their time slot, follow the ranger in line, help each other up the ladders and through the Balcony House tunnel; a shared picnic at the museum area; a campfire and evening programme at Morefield; sunset at Park Point. Open-world meeting spots: the Visitor and Research Center, the museum all-way stop and Spruce Tree Terrace, the Cliff Palace Overlook, the Wetherill kiosk.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): these are ancestral homes and sacred places to living peoples — enter cliff dwellings only with a ranger (or at Step House); never climb, sit or lean on walls; never enter kivas that are closed; don't touch plaster, paint or rock art; leave every pottery sherd and artifact where it lies (taking one is a crime and a loss); stay on trails and behind railings at overlooks; keep voices low; no drones; get off overlooks in lightning. The game should present the Ancestral Pueblo world through Pueblo voices, never as ruins to loot or dungeons to clear.
- **Implementation challenges**: alcoves (overhangs) and multi-storey masonry villages under a rock roof — 3D models set into cliff faces, with ladders, stairs and a crawl tunnel; timed tour slots and a guide NPC who leads a group; a cultural-heritage content layer written with the affiliated tribes; burned forests (snags, regrowth); a long winding entrance road; seasonal closures (Wetherill Mesa and the Cliff Palace Loop in winter).
