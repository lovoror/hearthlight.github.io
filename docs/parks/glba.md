# Glacier Bay National Park & Preserve — `GLBA`

> Alaska · established December 2, 1980 (Glacier Bay National Monument since February 26, 1925) · 13,044.6 km² (3,223,383 acres) of park, ~13,280 km² (3.28 million acres) with the Dry Bay preserve · 740,044 recreation visits (2025, mostly aboard cruise ships) ·
> 0–4,669 m · NPS region AKR (Alaska)
>
> A 65-mile (105 km) fjord that was solid ice in 1750, now opened by the fastest glacier retreat on record, ending in tidewater glaciers that thunder into the sea; the ancestral Homeland of the Huna and Yakutat Lingít. In Hearthlight it should feel like a rainy morning at a mossy cove, a Tribal House and a whale skeleton, then a boat ride up a green-to-grey fjord where the forest disappears, whales blow, and a wall of blue ice cracks and falls.

## Reading the map

The park map is wider than tall, north up. The park is a big green-edged block between the **Gulf of Alaska** (left and bottom-left, a long straight surf coast from Cape Spencer to Cape Fairweather and on to Dry Bay) and **Lynn Canal** / the Chilkat Range (right). A narrow green strip runs north-west up the coast to the **Dry Bay preserve** at the Alsek River mouth (ranger station, airstrips, public-use cabin, Alsek Lake with the Alsek Glacier), below the Brabazon Range. The park's spine is the white **Fairweather Range**, only 20–30 km from the ocean: Mount Fairweather (15,300 ft / 4,669 m) on the Canada border, Mount Quincy Adams, Mount Salisbury, Lituya Mountain, Mount Crillon and Mount La Perouse, with Lituya Bay cutting into the coast below them. North of the park lie British Columbia's Tatshenshini-Alsek Provincial Park and the Alsek Range; north-east the Takhinsha Mountains; south, across Icy Strait and Cross Sound, Chichagof Island and the Lingít village of **Hoonah**.

**Glacier Bay** itself is a Y-shaped fjord in the right half. It opens at the bottom onto **Icy Strait** between Point Gustavus and Point Carolus; the flat forested Gustavus foreland (town, airport) is at the bottom right, with the park's only road (red, 9.3 mi / 15 km) running north-west to **Bartlett Cove** (Visitor Center, Glacier Bay Lodge, campground). The lower bay is wide and island-dotted: the Beardslee Islands off Bartlett Cove, Sitakaday Narrows (a tidal race) between Point Gustavus and Point Carolus, then Strawberry, Willoughby, Francis, Drake and the South and North Marble islands; Geikie Inlet opens west, Beartrack Cove and North Sandy Cove east. At **Tlingit Point** the bay forks: the **East Arm** (Muir Inlet, with Adams, Wachusett and McBride inlets and the retreated Muir and Riggs glaciers) goes north; the **West Arm** bends north-west past Gilbert Peninsula, Hugh Miller Inlet, Blue Mouse Cove, Gloomy Knob and Tidal Inlet to Russell Island, where it splits again: **Johns Hopkins Inlet** west (Lamplugh, Johns Hopkins and Gilman glaciers) and **Tarr Inlet** north to **Margerie Glacier** and the **Grand Pacific Glacier** at the Canada border. Reid Inlet, Rendu Inlet and Queen Inlet are short side fjords. The **Brady Icefield** fills the peninsula between the bay and the ocean, with the Brady Glacier flowing south to Taylor Bay.

The map's most striking device is the **dated ice fronts**: thin blue lines labelled 1860, 1879, 1892, 1907, 1912, 1916, 1925, 1929, 1935, 1941, 1946, 1948, 1966, 1976 stepping up each arm, showing how the ice has withdrawn. The enlargement adds the oldest ones: « Glacier extent 1750–1780 » at Point Gustavus in Icy Strait, 1794 at the mouth of Bartlett Cove, 1845 at Spokane Cove, 1857 and 1860 near Tlingit Point, 1879 near Russell Island. It also marks « Interglacial stumps » (ancient forests uncovered by the retreat) near Wolf Point and at Fingers Bay, depth soundings (−1,416 ft / −432 m near Gloomy Knob, −198 ft / −60 m off Point Gustavus), and the peaks framing the bay: Mount Abdallah (5,964 ft), Mount Merriam (5,083 ft), Mount Wright (5,139 ft), Marble Mountain, Tlingit Peak, Mount Bulky, July Fourth Mountain, Blackthorn Peak, Serrated Peak, White Cap Mountain.

On the ground, everything a visitor walks is at **Bartlett Cove** (the campground map): the road arrives from Gustavus past park headquarters; the timber **Glacier Bay Lodge** (Visitor Center upstairs) sits above the beach; below it the **Tlingit Trail** runs along the shore past the humpback whale skeleton and a Lingít dugout canoe to **Xunaa Shuká Hít**, the Huna Ancestors' House; the **Visitor Information Station** stands at the head of the public dock (with the fuel dock beside it and a seaplane float), and the Healing Totem Pole next to it; the **Forest Trail** loops through spruce and hemlock past Blackwater Pond to the beach; the walk-in **campground** strings along the shore south of the docks in the « intertidal zone » with food caches, a warming shelter and a fire ring; trailheads lead north to the Bartlett River estuary and Bartlett Lake.

### Water

The whole park is organised by **salt water**: Glacier Bay is a drowned glacial valley, deep (over 400 m near Gloomy Knob) with a shallow **sill at the mouth** where the tides of Icy Strait race through Sitakaday Narrows. Near the glaciers the water is milky turquoise and full of floating ice (the icebergs « fizz » as their old air escapes — « bergie seltzer »); in the lower bay it is dark green and clear. Tides are large (up to ~7 m), exposing wide intertidal flats at Bartlett Cove, where campers must cook below the tide line. Glaciers: about 1,000 in the park; a handful are **tidewater** glaciers that calve into the sea — Margerie, Johns Hopkins (advancing), Lamplugh, Gilman, McBride — others reach the water only at high tide or have retreated onto land (Grand Pacific, Reid, Muir, Riggs). Freshwater: the Bartlett River and its estuary, Bartlett Lake, Blackwater Pond, Dundas and Excursion rivers; on the outer coast the Alsek River's 80-square-mile delta at Dry Bay and the surf of the Gulf of Alaska. Humpback whales feed in the lower bay all summer; « whale waters » rules limit boat speeds.

### Relief

From sea level to **Mount Fairweather, 4,669 m (15,300 ft)**, only ~20 km from the ocean: one of the steepest coast-to-summit rises on Earth. The bay's walls are **U-shaped**: near-vertical grey rock and icefalls in the upper arms, gentler forested slopes and low islands in the lower bay. The glacier faces are 60–75 m walls of ice above the water (plus a similar depth below). The land flattens only at Gustavus (a glacial outwash plain), at Dry Bay (delta and dunes) and on the outer-coast foreland. Because the land is rebounding after the ice (up to ~3 cm a year), beaches and meadows rise out of the sea. In the game: water everywhere, with cliff tiles for fjord walls (painted grey rock, waterfalls), bare-rock « young land » up-bay giving way to forest down-bay, glacier faces as tall 3D ice walls with crevasses and a calving system, icebergs as floating props, and the Fairweather Range as a row of 3D snow peaks along the western skyline.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation (and by age of the land)**: at Bartlett Cove (ice-free ~250 years) tall Sitka spruce and western hemlock rainforest with deep moss, devil's club, skunk cabbage in the bogs, blueberry and salmonberry; mid-bay (ice-free ~100–150 years) alder and willow thickets with young spruce; up-bay (ice-free decades) bare striated rock, gravel, Dryas mats, fireweed and lichens; above ~600 m alpine meadows, then rock, snow and ice. The trip up the bay is a trip back in time through plant succession.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#1F3B2D` old rainforest · `#6F8F4E` alder–willow thicket · `#B7B2A3` fresh glacial gravel · `#6E6C6A` fjord-wall rock · `#2F5D62` lower-bay green water · `#7FC8C7` upper-bay turquoise silt water · `#DDEFF7` glacier surface · `#5FA8D3` deep blue crevasse and calved-ice blue · `#8C7B6B` debris-covered ice · `#C8B98A` intertidal flats.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): **humpback whale** (lower bay and Icy Strait, June–September), orca, harbor porpoise, **Steller sea lion** (South Marble Island), **harbor seal** (pupping on the ice in Johns Hopkins Inlet, May–June), **sea otter** (back since the 1990s, now thousands in the lower bay), **mountain goat** (Gloomy Knob), brown and black bears (shorelines, the Bartlett River), moose (arrived in the 20th century), wolf, coyote, porcupine, red squirrel; birds — **tufted and horned puffins** and black-legged kittiwakes (Marble Islands), bald eagle, pigeon guillemot, marbled and Kittlitz's murrelets (near the ice), Arctic tern, Canada geese at the estuary.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): mountain avens (Dryas, the first coloniser, June), fireweed (July–August), Nootka lupine (beaches, June), beach strawberry (Strawberry Island, June), skunk cabbage (bogs, April–May), devil's club (forest), chocolate lily (June), salmonberry and blueberry (July–August), Sitka alder (nitrogen fixer), Sitka spruce and western hemlock.
- **Weather & seasons**: rain is normal, low clouds and fog lift to reveal the Fairweathers; April–June are the driest months, September–October the wettest; cold katabatic winds and ice near the glaciers even in July; winter is dark, wet and snowy but ice-free at the cove.
- **Night**: ~18.3 h of sun at the June solstice at Bartlett Cove (sunrise ~3:54, sunset ~22:14 AKDT), long blue twilight; aurora from late August on clear nights; campers hear whales blow in the dark (« hear whales while you sleep »).
- **Sounds**: glacier calving — a crack like a rifle shot, then a rolling « white thunder » and a surge; the fizz of melting bergs; humpback blows and trumpeting; sea lions roaring on South Marble; kittiwakes; rain drumming on spruce; the day boat's engines; drums and songs at the Tribal House.

## Moments

- **Calving at Margerie Glacier**: blocks of ice up to ~200 ft high fall from a face ~245 ft above the water; the day boat lingers in front of it around midday.
- **The day boat** (late May–early September): board 6:50 am, back ~3:30 pm, a ranger aboard; Junior Rangers are sworn in on the boat.
- **Humpback whales** return each summer (June–September) to feed in the lower bay; bubble-net feeding and breaching in Icy Strait.
- **Harbor seal pupping** on the icebergs of Johns Hopkins Inlet (May–June; the inlet is closed to boats then).
- **Glacial history**: in 1750 one glacier filled the bay; in 1794 Vancouver's crews found an ice front near Bartlett Cove; in 1879 John Muir found it ~48 mi up-bay; today it is ~65 mi (105 km) back. The Lingít remember villages overrun by the ice in the early 1700s.
- **Xunaa Shuká Hít** (dedicated 2016) and the new totem poles — Our Grandparents' Ocean Totem Pole greeting boats and Our Grandparents' Land Totem Pole at the park entrance (raised August 22, 2025); tribal programs and a canoe tradition revived in the Homeland.
- **Ranger programs**: daily Forest Trail walk at 1:30 pm, evening programs at the lodge, tribal interpreters at the Tribal House (summer).
- **Low tide at Bartlett Cove**: the flats open, tidepools with sea stars and anemones, campers cook on the beach below the tide line.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a curated map of **~1,280 × 1,024 tiles**, north up, with the bay as a diagonal ribbon of water from Bartlett Cove (bottom right) to Tarr Inlet (top left). (1) **Bartlett Cove** ~420 × 300 tiles near true scale (the real cove area is ~1.5 × 1 km: lodge, VIS, docks, Tribal House, campground, Forest and Tlingit trails, the Bartlett River estuary); (2) **Gustavus** ~160 × 120 tiles (airport, ferry dock, the Land Totem Pole) joined by a compressed stretch of the 10-mile road; (3) **the bay**, compressed about 1:50 (105 km → ~1,400 tiles of water along the diagonal), with stop points at true-ish scale: Sitakaday Narrows (a current), Beardslee Islands, South Marble Island, Tlingit Point, Gloomy Knob, Russell Island, Lamplugh, Johns Hopkins Inlet (a short dead-end), Reid Inlet (a walk-up glacier), and at the head **Margerie & Grand Pacific** as a true-scale ice wall (the ~1.6 km face compressed ×2). Cut: the outer coast, Lituya Bay, the East Arm (a later kayak expansion), Dry Bay.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: Glacier Bay Lodge (a big timber lodge with a fireplace lobby and the Visitor Center upstairs), the Visitor Information Station at the dock head, the public dock and fuel dock with the catamaran, **Xunaa Shuká Hít** (a cedar clan house with a painted façade; interior with carved house screen and four clan house posts — built and described with the Hoonah Indian Association's own words), totem poles, the dugout canoe, the 45-ft **humpback whale skeleton**, the campground's food caches and warming shelter. 3D landmarks: the tidewater glacier faces (Margerie, Lamplugh, Johns Hopkins), Mount Fairweather and the Fairweather Range, Gloomy Knob, the Marble Islands, cruise ships as moving landmarks.
- **Activities**: ride the day boat (the whole party on deck, a ranger narrating; spot whales, sea lions, puffins, goats and bears for the field guide), **sea kayaking** (rent at the cove; paddle the Beardslee Islands; a drop-off up-bay for an expedition), shore walks and tidepooling at low tide, the Forest Trail ranger walk, Tribal House programs, ranger evening talks, a Junior Ranger « Adventure Guide » sworn in aboard the boat, whale-listening (a hydrophone mini-game: the park has recorded underwater sound for decades), photo spots — Margerie calving, South Marble puffins, Gloomy Knob goats, the whale skeleton, the Healing Totem Pole, Bartlett Cove at dusk, Point Gustavus; fishing (licence) and camping on island beaches.
- **Passport stamps**: « Gustavus » (Glacier Bay Visitor Center in the lodge and the Visitor Information Station at the dock) and « Dry Bay Ranger Station » (the preserve).
- **With friends / with the world**: the day boat is a natural shared ride for strangers (150 seats, one departure a day, everyone rushing to the rail when the ice falls); co-op kayaking (a tandem kayak, one steers, one paddles; crossing Sitakaday Narrows at slack tide together); a party camp on an island beach with a shared food cache; gathering spots — the lodge's fireplace lobby on rainy days, the dock at boat time, the Tribal House for stories; group photo in front of Margerie Glacier from the boat's bow.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): stay ¼ nautical mile from humpback whales (never approach within ½ nm) and from seals on ice, 100 yards from hauled-out sea lions; « whale waters » speed limits for all boats; no feeding wildlife; campers cook and eat only in the intertidal zone and store food in caches or bear canisters (never in a kayak); fires only in the fire ring or warming shelter; closures around seal pupping and bird nesting areas; kayakers keep well back from calving faces (waves and falling ice); orientation at the VIS before any overnight trip; respect the Tribal House and Lingít cultural sites.
- **Implementation challenges**: a map that is mostly sea (open water, tides that move the shoreline, currents at the narrows); tidewater glacier faces with scheduled and random calving, splash waves and floating icebergs that drift and melt; boats (a large NPC catamaran on a route, cruise ships, player kayaks) with speed limits near whales; swimming whales that surface and blow; steep fjord walls; the « back in time » vegetation gradient along the bay; rain and fog as the default weather; respectful representation of Lingít clan houses and poles.
