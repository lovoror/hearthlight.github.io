# Congaree National Park — `CONG`

> South Carolina · established November 10, 2003 (Congaree Swamp National Monument: October 18, 1976) · 108.0 km² (26,693
> acres) · 287,833 recreation visits (2025) · 24–43 m · NPS region SER (today DOI Region 2, South Atlantic–Gulf)
>
> The largest intact old-growth bottomland hardwood forest left in the southeastern United States: a flat, flooding river
> forest of giant cypress, tupelo and loblolly pine, where the Congaree River spills through the trees about ten times a year.
> In Hearthlight it should feel like a hushed green cathedral you explore by boardwalk and canoe, where the water level is the
> weather, and where the fireflies blink in unison on late-spring nights.

## Reading the map

North up, the park is a long, flat strip lying west–east along the **north bank of the Congaree River**, which forms most of its south edge before joining the Wateree River just past the eastern tip (together they become the Santee). The northern edge follows a low **bluff** — the only dry high ground, about 15 m above the floodplain — with Old Bluff Road and farmland above it. Everything south of the bluff is floodplain: a maze of old-growth forest, winding **Cedar Creek**, stagnant sloughs, muddy "guts" (small channels) and crescent **oxbow lakes** left by the river's old loops.

All visitor facilities sit in one small cluster on the bluff at the **west end**: from Old Bluff Road, National Park Road runs 2 km south past **Longleaf Campground** to the **Harry Hampton Visitor Center**. From there the **Boardwalk loop** (2.6 mi) drops off the bluff into the forest — an elevated section first, then a low section — reaching **Weston Lake** at its far (southern) end. The numbered foot trails radiate from the visitor centre and boardwalk: the **Bluff** and **Firefly** trails stay on high ground, **Sims** and **Weston Lake** reach Cedar Creek, **Oakridge** and **Boggy Gut** (formerly River Trail) push south across the floodplain to the Congaree River and its sandbar, and **Kingsnake** runs east along Cedar Creek to **South Cedar Creek Landing**, the canoe put-in reached by its own road.

**Cedar Creek** enters at **Bannister Bridge** (north-west corner, mile 0 of the canoe trail), winds south-east past Wise Lake and Weston Lake to South Cedar Creek Landing (mile 6.6, an iron bridge), then continues ~6.5 mi east through the wildest part of the park to **Mazyck's Cut**, where it enters the Congaree. The **eastern section** is a separate pocket around US 601 (shown in an inset): Bates Ferry Trail follows a 1920s ferry road to the river, Fork Swamp Trail skirts **Bates Old River**, an oxbow cut off by a hurricane in 1852, and Bates Bridge Boat Landing is the take-out for river trips. Between the two ends lies roadless wilderness (over 20,000 acres designated in 1988 and after), dotted with 19th-century **cattle mounds** — earthen refuges raised for livestock during floods.

### Water

Water defines Congaree. The **Congaree River** — formed at Columbia by the Broad and Saluda rivers — runs along the south edge, wide, muddy and slow, and joins the **Wateree** at the east end to form the Santee. About **ten times a year** it overtops its banks and "pulses" water through the forest, laying down the silt and nutrients that grow the giant trees; floods are most common from winter to spring and can come with little warning. **Cedar Creek**, a blackwater stream stained tea-dark by tannins, winds through the middle of the park; its level can swing by 10 ft (3 m), flood stage is 8 ft on the park gauge, and at low water it becomes a chain of logjams to portage. Between them lie **oxbow lakes** (Weston, Wise, Dawson's, Big Lake, Bates Old River — the last cut off by an 1852 hurricane), **sloughs** full of cypress knees and **guts** (small channels such as Boggy Gut, Running Gut, Tennessee Gut) that fill and drain with every flood. Surface water is rarely more than a metre or two deep outside the channels, but it covers huge areas; a high-water line of mud on the trunks shows the last flood.

### Relief

Almost none: from **24 m** at the river to **43 m** on the bluff — the whole floodplain is within a few metres of level, so *height is expressed as water*. The only real "slope" is the **bluff** at the north edge (~15 m), where the boardwalk and trails step down into the forest. Inside the floodplain, relief is measured in tens of centimetres: slightly higher **ridges** of old natural levees (Oakridge), lower **sloughs** and **guts**, the **cattle mounds** (a couple of metres high), creek banks and the river's cut bank. In the game this is the park's big opportunity: use only 2–4 height levels (bluff, levee ridges and mounds, forest floor, slough/channel) and let a **variable water level** decide what is land — at low water most of the floor is walkable mud, at mid water the sloughs and guts fill and trails are cut, at flood only the bluff, the elevated boardwalk and the mounds stay dry and canoes can glide between the trunks.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation** (by centimetres here): the **bluff** (40 m) is open, fire-kept pine woods of loblolly and longleaf pine with fox squirrels; the **levee ridges and higher floodplain** carry the champion forest — loblolly pines up to ~51 m tall, cherrybark and other bottomland oaks, sweetgum, American elm, hickory, American holly, with a thick understory of **dwarf palmetto** and switchcane; the **sloughs and oxbows** are **bald cypress and water tupelo** standing in water, their swollen trunks and **cypress knees** poking up, draped with Spanish moss and resurrection fern. The canopy averages over 30 m, among the highest of any temperate deciduous forest; fallen giants (many from Hurricane Hugo in 1989) lie across the floor.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#3B2A1A` tannin-dark Cedar Creek water · `#8A7355` flood mud and silt on the trunks · `#5A4632` wet leaf litter · `#7D6A55` cypress bark (fluted, grey-brown) · `#9C8F7A` cypress knees · `#4F7A3A` palmetto fronds · `#2F4A2C` deep canopy shade · `#B5C27A` spring leaf-out green · `#A0522D` cypress needles in November · `#C9B98C` river sandbar.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): **synchronous firefly** *Photuris frontalis* (mid-May to mid-June) · **river otter** (Cedar Creek, Weston Lake) · **white-tailed deer** · **bobcat** · **feral hog** (invasive rooting) · **barred owl** (the voice of the swamp) · **pileated woodpecker** and **red-headed woodpecker** (big dead trees) · **prothonotary warbler** (golden, nesting in cavities over the water, April–July) · **wild turkey** · **wood duck** · **great blue heron** and white ibis in the sloughs · **American alligator** (occasional, in the creek) · **cottonmouth**, **canebrake rattlesnake** and **copperhead** · **yellow-bellied slider** (on logs) · **eastern box turtle** · the **mosquito** (famous, rated daily on the VC's Mosquito Meter).
- **Flora** (the herbarium): bald cypress (knees; rusty needles in November) · water tupelo (swollen bases, dark fruit in autumn) · loblolly pine (champion giants) · cherrybark oak · sweetgum (star leaves, red in fall) · dwarf palmetto (the evergreen understory) · switchcane (native giant cane) · Spanish moss · resurrection fern (curls up dry, greens after rain) · atamasco lily (white, March–April, damp floodplain).
- **Weather & seasons**: spring (March–May) is green and loud with birds and frogs, floods still common, fireflies from mid-May; summer is hot (often 32–35 °C), sticky, stormy and mosquito-thick, with low water and exposed sandbars late in the season; autumn is dry and mild, cypress needles turn orange-rust in November; winter is cool and bare, the best time for high water and "floating forest" floods. Fog lies in the floodplain on cool mornings. Hurricanes and storms regularly topple trees and close trails.
- **Night**: fireflies — the synchronous *Photuris frontalis* display in late spring, plus other firefly species in summer; barred owls calling back and forth; frog choruses (bullfrogs, green tree frogs, spring peepers); dark sky through canopy gaps and over the river sandbar; the glow of Columbia to the north-west.
- **Sounds**: barred owls ("who cooks for you?"), woodpecker drumming on hollow trees, the hollow thud of feet on the boardwalk, dripping water, a paddle knocking a cypress knee, frogs and cicadas, the buzz of mosquitoes, wind high in the canopy while the floor stays still, the crack and crash of a falling limb in a storm.

## Moments

- **Synchronous fireflies**: for about two weeks between mid-May and mid-June, male *Photuris frontalis* flash in near-unison along the creek and in the lowland forest from dusk into the night; the park manages viewing on peak nights (the Firefly Trail; in recent years parking has been limited by lottery or pass — check each spring).
- **Floods**: about ten times a year the Congaree tops its banks and the forest becomes a shallow lake for days; the low boardwalk and most trails close, while the elevated boardwalk stays open above the water.
- **Low water**: late summer and autumn expose the river sandbar and turn Cedar Creek into a chain of logs to portage.
- **Spring chorus and migration** (April–May): prothonotary warblers arrive, frogs call, the forest leafs out.
- **Autumn in the cypress**: November, cypress needles turn rust and sweetgum red.
- **Full-moon paddles**: outfitters run moonlit canoe trips on Cedar Creek.
- **The Mosquito Meter**: the VC's daily sign rating the bugs from "all clear" upward — a summer ritual photo.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a **1024 × 512-tile** vignette map, keeping the real proportions (25 × 11 km compressed ~15×). Near true scale (1 tile ≈ 1.5 m): the visitor centre and its lots, the start of the boardwalk and one full "champion tree" grove; compressed ×3: the Boardwalk loop (4.2 km → ~900 tiles of boardwalk, elevated and low sections kept distinct), Weston Lake, South Cedar Creek Landing; heavily compressed: the long Kingsnake and Oakridge trails, the deep floodplain, the eastern section (moved closer, as a small area at the east edge with US 601 and the oxbow). The creek keeps its winding course; the river runs along the bottom edge.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: the only hub is the **Harry Hampton Visitor Center** (interior with exhibits and the Mosquito Meter outside), with the campgrounds nearby and **South Cedar Creek Landing** (iron bridge, canoe racks) as a second, smaller one. 3D landmarks: the champion loblolly pines and cypresses (a few "giant" tree models towering over the instanced forest), the **elevated boardwalk** on posts, fallen giants you walk around or under, cypress knee fields, the cattle mounds, the iron bridge, Bates Ferry's old road cut.
- **Activities**: the **flood** as the park's core mechanic — a water level that rises and falls on a cycle (several floods per in-game season, announced by rangers and the creek gauge): what is a trail today is a canoe route tomorrow, the low boardwalk vanishes, animals move to the mounds, debris lines and fresh mud mark the retreat, new sandbars and logjams appear; **canoe and kayak** the Cedar Creek trail (portage over logs at low water, paddle between trunks at high water, find the trail markers); walk the boardwalk with the self-guided stops; find and measure the **champion trees** (a herbarium quest: tallest loblolly, biggest cypress, tupelo); **firefly night** on the Firefly Trail; owl calls at dusk; fishing in the oxbow lakes; backcountry camping (permit) and a river-sandbar camp on the Blue Trail. Photo spots: the boardwalk among giant trunks, cypress knees in Weston Lake, the forest in flood, the iron bridge at South Cedar Creek Landing, the river sandbar, the Mosquito Meter. Junior ranger ideas (the real booklet is at the VC or printable): read the flood gauge and predict which trails will close, count cypress knees, identify five trees by their bark, learn why the forest needs floods, match bird calls (barred owl, pileated woodpecker, prothonotary warbler).
- **Passport stamps**: the **Harry Hampton Visitor Center**, the park's only visitor facility.
- **With friends / with the world**: the boardwalk is the shared promenade where everyone passes; South Cedar Creek Landing is where canoe groups gather; firefly night is the event of the year (a dark trail, whispering crowds, lights off). Co-op: tandem canoes (one steers, one spots trail markers and logs), a group portage lifting the canoe over a log, a flood-day rescue of a stranded hiker on a cattle mound, a champion-tree hunt where players must hold hands to measure the girth.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): check the flood gauge and alerts before heading out (and turn back when water covers the trail); stay on the boardwalk and marked trails (numbered blazes every 0.1 mi); no bikes or e-bikes on trails; no fires in the backcountry; don't carve, cut or climb the trees; keep your distance from alligators and snakes; lights off (or red) and stay on the trail at the firefly viewing; pack out everything; wear a PFD on the creek; bring repellent — the Mosquito Meter is not a joke.
- **Implementation challenges**: a **dynamic water level** over a near-flat map (water surfaces that rise over the forest floor, trails and boardwalk sections that submerge, canoe navigation among trunks, mud and debris after floods); very **tall trees** in a low-resolution top-down view (the height of the canopy must read); dense understory (palmetto, cane, knees) without hiding the paths; floods and logjams changing the canoe route; fireflies flashing in sync across the whole forest; a humid, hazy, green-gold light under the canopy.
