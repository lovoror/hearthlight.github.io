# Kenai Fjords National Park — `KEFJ`

> Alaska · established December 2, 1980 (national monument since December 1, 1978) · 2,710.0 km² (669,650 acres) · 425,369 recreation visits (2025) ·
> 0–1,966 m · NPS region AKR (Alaska)
>
> The Harding Icefield and the ~40 glaciers that spill from it, some down to a forested valley at the end of a road near Seward, others straight into drowned fjords full of whales, sea lions and puffins. In Hearthlight it should feel like two days in one: a morning walk up a valley the ice left behind — past dated signs of its retreat — to the edge of a white desert, then a rocking boat ride out of Resurrection Bay to a blue glacier face.

## Reading the map

The brochure map is nearly square, north up. The park is a green-outlined wedge on the south-east flank of the **Kenai Mountains**, whose centre is the white **Harding Icefield** (the label runs diagonally across the middle). Its outline follows the icefield's divide on the north-west (beyond it: the Kenai National Wildlife Refuge, Tustumena Lake and the Tustumena Glacier) and drops to the **Gulf of Alaska** on the south-east, where the coast is shredded into fjords, peninsulas and islands. The town of **Seward** sits outside the park at the top right, at the head of **Resurrection Bay**, with the Seward Highway (route 9, red) coming down from Anchorage past Bear Lake and the Lost Lake and Primrose Creek trails.

From Seward, the **Herman Leirer (Exit Glacier) Road** — the park's only road — runs west-north-west up the braided **Resurrection River** valley to the **Exit Glacier Nature Center** (campground and the winter-only Willow Public Use Cabin just before it), where Exit Glacier tumbles out of the icefield. The dashed Resurrection River Trail (a US Forest Service cabin on it) continues north. South of Seward, the Coastal Trail follows the west shore of Resurrection Bay past Lowell Point and Tonsina Point to Caines Head State Recreation Area; across the bay rise the Resurrection Peninsula, Fox Island, Rugged Island, Cape Resurrection and Barwell Island.

The park's coast is read left-to-right as a row of glacial fjords opening south-east. Nearest to Seward, **Bear Glacier** (the park's largest) ends in an iceberg-filled lagoon behind a sand spit west of Callisto Head. Then **Aialik Bay**, the classic boat-tour destination: the Aialik Peninsula on its east side (landing beaches, Bulldog Cove, Coleman Bay, Three Hole Point), Slate Island and the Aialik public-use cabin near its head, **Aialik Glacier** at the top, the **Holgate Arm** branching west to **Holgate Glacier** (Holgate public-use cabin and the Aialik Bay Ranger Station at its mouth), then Cheval Island, Chat Island, No Name Island and Aialik Cape guarding the entrance. West of the Harris Peninsula lies **Harris Bay** with the long **Northwestern Fjord** reaching north to Northwestern Glacier (a dated 1900 ice front sits at the fjord mouth); then Two Arm Bay, Paguna and Taroka arms, the **McCarty Fjord** (ice fronts dated 1905, 1926 and 1942 far down the fjord show a 20-km retreat) and finally **Nuka Bay**, with its old gold mines, the Nuka River and the Pye Islands. Offshore, the **Chiswell Islands** and the Pye Islands belong to the Alaska Maritime National Wildlife Refuge.

Relief is labelled with spot heights: the unnamed « Highest point in park » (6,450 ft) near the head of Northwestern Glacier; Phoenix Peak (5,155 ft) above Exit Glacier; Mount Marathon (« Marathon Mountain », 4,603 ft) behind Seward; Mount Alice (5,265 ft) across the bay. Water depths are marked in the fjords (−972 ft / −296 m in Aialik Bay, −1,032 ft / −315 m in Nuka Bay). The legend box at the bottom has the scale bar (0–5 km / 0–5 mi), a « For Your Safety » note, and a « Native Corporation Land » note: private lands owned by Port Graham Corporation, an Alaska Native (Sugpiaq/Alutiiq) corporation, are shown along the outer coast and may only be used with permits.

### Water

The park's waters are **fjords**: glacier-carved valleys drowned by the sea and by the sinking of the coast (the Pacific Plate dives under the Kenai here; the 1964 earthquake dropped the shore further), with floors 180–300 m below sea level and shallower moraine sills at their mouths. Meltwater loaded with **glacial flour** colours the inner fjords jade-grey; the open Gulf of Alaska beyond **Harding Gateway** is deep blue, often rough, with big swells. Tides are large (~4–5 m). Tidewater glaciers still reach the sea at the heads of Aialik Bay (Aialik), Holgate Arm (Holgate) and Northwestern Fjord; others (Bear, Pederson, McCarty) now end in lagoons or on land. On land, **Exit Creek** braids across a grey outwash plain below Exit Glacier and joins the Resurrection River, which runs to Resurrection Bay at Seward; lakes include Bear Glacier Lagoon, Bear Lake and, in the south-west, Delight and Desire lakes; Kvasnikoff Falls drops into Nuka Bay. The Harding Icefield receives ~60 ft (18 m) of snow a year and feeds about 40 glaciers; 51 % of the park is ice.

### Relief

From sea level to the unnamed 1,966 m (6,450 ft) summit on the icefield, all packed into 30–40 km. The icefield is a gently domed white plateau at ~1,000–1,900 m pierced by rock **nunataks**; glaciers fall off it in steep icefalls to classic **U-shaped valleys**. At Exit Glacier the land steps: the flat outwash plain (~100–120 m), the rocky snout and overlook knob (~170 m), a steep forested slope, Marmot Meadows (~510 m), the cliffs (~740 m), then rock and snow to the icefield rim (~1,070 m). On the coast, mountains plunge straight into the sea as cliffs and sea stacks; flat ground exists only on beaches, spits and outwash fans. In the game: water and cliff tiles along the fjords, an icefield plateau with nunatak peaks as 3D rock landmarks, a steep, switchbacked trail with stairs, the outwash plain as braided shallow channels, and glacier tongues as tall ice ramps with crevasse patterns.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation (and by time since the ice)**: along the Exit Glacier road the forest gets younger towards the ice — Sitka spruce and hemlock near the park entrance, then black cottonwood, Sitka alder and willow on the 20th-century moraines, then Dryas, dwarf fireweed, mosses and lichens on bare gravel near the snout. Treeline is low (~230–300 m); above it alder thickets, lupine and fireweed meadows (Marmot Meadows), heather and dwarf willow, then rock, snow and ice. The outer coast has spruce–hemlock rainforest on the islands and fjord walls, with devil's club and salmonberry.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#F1F5F8` icefield snow · `#9CC4DA` glacier blue ice · `#6B6A66` dark nunatak and fjord-wall rock · `#B9B3A6` outwash gravel · `#8FA7A0` glacial-flour fjord water · `#23465A` open Gulf water · `#1E3A2B` spruce–hemlock rainforest · `#7FA05A` cottonwood–alder green · `#C95F9A` fireweed magenta · `#E8D9B5` summer grass on the meadows.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): humpback whale (feeding in the fjords late spring–summer), orca, fin and minke whales, gray whale (spring migration in Resurrection Bay), Dall's porpoise, **Steller sea lion** (Chiswell Islands, Aialik Cape), **harbor seal** (on ice floes near the glaciers), **sea otter**, **mountain goat** (fjord cliffs and above Exit Glacier), black bear (Exit Glacier valley, berries in August) and brown bear, moose (Exit Glacier road, especially in winter), hoary marmot (Marmot Meadows), porcupine; birds — **tufted and horned puffins**, common murre, black-legged kittiwake, bald eagle, peregrine falcon, marbled and Kittlitz's murrelets, Steller's jay in the forest.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): yellow Dryas and dwarf fireweed (first colonisers on new gravel), fireweed (July–August), Nootka lupine (June–July), cow parsnip, salmonberry (July), devil's club, Sitka alder (nitrogen-fixer), black cottonwood (the new valley forest), mountain heather (July), lichens on the nunataks.
- **Weather & seasons**: late spring (May) brings migrating gray whales and snow still on the icefield trail; June–August are the calmest seas and long days but often rain and low cloud; September storms and fall colours on the alders; the Exit Glacier road closes to cars around November; winter is deep snow on the icefield (from September) and cold, dark days in the valley, the best time for moose and aurora.
- **Night**: ~18.9 h of sun at Exit Glacier on the June solstice (sunrise ~4:31, sunset ~23:28 AKDT) and bright twilight all night; aurora on clear winter nights over the valley; the icefield glows blue under a full moon.
- **Sounds**: meltwater thundering under the ice at the Edge of the Glacier, gravel clacking in Exit Creek, hoary marmots' whistles, the crack and boom of calving at Holgate Glacier, sea lions roaring on the Chiswells, puffin and kittiwake colonies, a humpback's blow, boat engines and the captain's commentary, rain on a spruce forest.

## Moments

- **Calving at Holgate or Aialik Glacier**: boats idle in front of the face while chunks fall with a delayed boom; harbor seals rest on the floating ice.
- **Whales in the fjords**: humpbacks feeding (late May–September), resident and transient orcas, gray whales migrating past Resurrection Bay in spring (March–May).
- **Seabird summer** (June–August): puffins and murres on the Chiswell Islands and Cape Resurrection cliffs.
- **Reaching the icefield**: the last switchbacks of the Harding Icefield Trail and a white horizon from the overlook; snow lingers on the upper trail into July.
- **The dated moraines of Exit Glacier**: signs along the road and trails mark where the ice stood in past years, showing its steady retreat (it lost ~57 m in 2013–2014 alone).
- **Ranger programs** (summer 2026): Exit Glacier walks at 10 am and 2 pm (May 23–September 7), pavilion talks at 1:30 pm, Junior Ranger walks on Tuesdays, Thursdays and Saturdays at 11 am, Fjord Junior Ranger walks at the Seward visitor center on Wednesdays and Sundays at 3 pm.
- **Winter at Exit Glacier** (~November–May): the road becomes a snow trail for skiers, mushers and snowmachines; a warming hut, the Willow cabin, and ice climbing on the snout (November–March).
- **Seward's Fourth of July**: the Mount Marathon race runs up and down the peak above town, a gateway tradition just outside the park.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a curated map of **~1,024 × 1,024 tiles**, north up, in two linked parts. (1) **Exit Glacier valley** ~480 × 400 tiles in the north-east quarter, near true scale around the nature center, the Glacier View loop, the outwash plain and the snout (the real area is ~1.2 × 1 km), with the Harding Icefield Trail compressed ~2× (6.6 km one-way → ~2,200 tiles of path, climbing ~950 m as a staircase of terraces) up to an icefield-edge plateau; the 8.6-mile road compressed into a short drive from (2) **Seward** (~200 × 160 tiles: small boat harbor, visitor center, a slice of town under Mount Marathon). (3) **The fjords**, compressed ~1:40, occupying the southern two-thirds: Resurrection Bay (Caines Head, Fox Island, Bear Glacier lagoon, Cape Resurrection), the open-water crossing of Harding Gateway, the Chiswell Islands, **Aialik Bay** with Holgate Arm at true-ish scale (the Holgate face ~1 km wide compressed ×2), the ranger station and a cabin beach. Cut: Northwestern, McCarty and Nuka fjords (optional later), the icefield interior (backdrop only).
- **Hubs & set pieces**: Seward's small boat harbor (docks, tour boats, the visitor center), Exit Glacier Nature Center and pavilion, the dated moraine signposts, the glacier snout with blue crevasses and a meltwater portal, Marmot Meadows, the stone emergency shelter at the trail's end, the Holgate and Aialik tidewater faces, the sea stacks of the Chiswell Islands, the Aialik and Holgate public-use cabins, the Aialik Bay ranger cabin. 3D landmarks: Exit Glacier's icefall, Phoenix Peak, Mount Marathon, nunataks poking out of the icefield, Bear Glacier with its two medial moraines.
- **Activities**: hike the Glacier View loop and the Harding Icefield Trail (a stamina challenge with rest points), photo spots — Glacier View, Glacier Overlook, Top of the Cliffs, icefield overlook, Holgate calving, puffins on the Chiswells, a sea lion haul-out, Bear Glacier lagoon by kayak; a boat tour (shared ride with wildlife spotting and a calving wait); sea kayaking among icebergs; a public-use cabin night; ranger walks and pavilion talks; Junior Ranger journal and the Fjord Junior Ranger patch at the harbor; winter mode — ski, snowshoe, dog-sled or fat-bike the closed road to the snowy glacier and a warming hut; flightseeing over the icefield.
- **Passport stamps**: « Seward » (Kenai Fjords National Park Visitor Center, Small Boat Harbor), « Exit Glacier » and « Exit Glacier Nature Center » (at the nature center).
- **With friends / with the world**: the icefield trail as a group climb with a summit photo at the overlook; a boat tour as a shared ride for strangers (one boat, many decks, everyone runs to the rail for a whale); tandem kayaks in Aialik Bay; a party camp at a landing beach; gathering spots — the harbor boardwalk before departures, the nature center plaza, the icefield overlook, Holgate Glacier's viewing area on the water.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): stay on trails in the Exit Glacier valley (fragile new soils); keep back from the glacier's edge and never walk on or under the ice (falling ice, meltwater surges); stay well away from calving faces by boat or kayak (waves); give marine mammals room (at least 100 yards; seals on ice flush easily) and never feed wildlife; store food against bears at campsites and cabins; no pets on trails; respect Port Graham Corporation lands (permits) and cultural sites on the coast; check tides before shore walks.
- **Implementation challenges**: a glacier that visibly retreats (dated positions, maybe a time slider), glacier ice with crevasses and a snout you approach but cannot enter; an icefield plateau (snow as ground, whiteout weather); braided outwash channels; tidewater calving with waves and floating ice; open-sea boat travel with swell and seasickness-free camera motion; sea stacks, arches and seabird colonies; whales and sea lions animated at sea; rain as the default; a winter season with the road turned into a trail.
