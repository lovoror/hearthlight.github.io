# National Park of American Samoa — `NPSA`

> American Samoa · established October 31, 1988 · 33.4 km² (8,257 acres, about half of it under water) · 43,258 recreation
> visits (2025) · 0–966 m · NPS region PWR (today DOI Region 12, Pacific Islands)
>
> The only US national park south of the equator: rain-forested volcanic ridges falling into coral reefs on three islands —
> Tutuila, Ofu and Taʻū — 2,600 miles south-west of Hawaiʻi, on land the park leases from Samoan villages. In Hearthlight it
> should feel like being a guest: flying foxes gliding over the ridges at noon, white sand and turquoise lagoons, village
> fale with open sides, and a culture — faʻasamoa — that sets the rhythm of the day.

## Reading the map

The park is **three separate pieces** in a line across the ocean, each drawn as a pale green « authorized park area » on its own island map, with turquoise **coral reefs** hugging every shore (the park's marine areas extend over the reefs). West to east:

**Tutuila unit** (the largest and the one most visitors see). North up, Tutuila is a long, narrow, steep island ~30 km east–west, bent around the deep, fjord-like **Pago Pago Harbor** that bites into its south coast from the south-east. The park is a green block on the **north coast**, directly behind the harbor: from **Fagasā Bay** in the west it runs east along the ridge crest over **Mount ʻAlava** (491 m, with its communications tower) and down to the north shore at **Vatia** village and its bay, where **Pola Island** — a 128 m sea stack of sheer cliffs — stands off the point, then on east through the **Amalau Valley** to **Āfono** Bay. The only roads are the coastal Route 001 around the harbor, Route 005 over **Fagasā Pass** to Fagasā, and Route 006 over **Āfono Pass** and down the north coast to Vatia; the park itself is roadless except where 006 skirts it. The **Mount ʻAlava Trail** follows the old ridge road from Fagasā Pass to the summit; the **Mount ʻAlava Adventure Trail** descends the other side on 56 ladders to Vatia; short trails reach Pola Island and the Lower Sauma Ridge star mound. The **National Park Visitor Center** is in Pago Pago village at the head of the harbor; the WWII gun batteries of **Blunts Point** and **Breakers Point** guard the harbor mouth outside the park. The airport is on the flat south-west plain (Tafuna), and the other island landmarks — Rainmaker Mountain (North Pioa, 523 m) above the harbor, Matafao Peak (653 m, the island's highest), Fagatele Bay marine sanctuary on the south-west coast — frame the picture.

**Ofu unit** (~100 km east). The Manuʻa map shows two small twin islands, **Ofu** and **Olosega**, joined by a bridge over the narrow Āsaga Strait. The park is only a thin green strip on Ofu's south-east shore — **Ofu Beach**, a long white-sand beach and the lagoon and reef in front of it — between the Vaʻoto airstrip area (with the park's **Ofu ranger station**) and the strait, under the forested Leʻolo Ridge and Sunuʻitao Peak; Tumu Mountain (494 m) is Ofu's summit, Piumafua (639 m) Olosega's. An unpaved road runs round Ofu's south coast and over the bridge to Olosega village.

**Taʻū unit** (~10 km further south-east). Taʻū is a round, high island; the park covers its entire **south-east half**, from the summit plateau of **Lata Mountain** (966 m, the highest point in American Samoa) and the craters Olotania and Luatele (Judds Crater) down the Liu Bench and the deep Laufuti valley to the enormous **south sea cliffs**, and along the east coast to Fitiʻuta. An unpaved road runs along the north coast from the villages of Faleāsao, Luma and Taʻū in the west to **Fitiʻuta** and its airstrip in the north-east; from Fitiʻuta a trail continues down the east coast past the **Saua** site (sacred in Samoan tradition) to **Siʻu Point** at the south-east tip. The interior is trackless cloud forest.

### Water

- **The reefs** are the park's second half: about 4,000 acres of the park are under water, fringing reefs and lagoons off all three islands. They hold roughly 900 fish species, hundreds of corals, giant clams, sea turtles and reef sharks. **Ofu's lagoon** is famous for its colourful, heat-tolerant corals in shallow backreef pools. Humpback whales visit in the southern winter (about July/August–October).
- **Pago Pago Harbor** is one of the deepest natural harbors in the South Pacific, a drowned volcanic valley; it is outside the park.
- **Streams and falls** run short and steep from the ridges (Laufuti Falls on Taʻū's south coast, the Amalau stream on Tutuila); no lakes.
- **Coast**: rugged volcanic shores with sea stacks (Pola, Fatu Rock), boulder and coral-rubble beaches, a few white-sand beaches (Ofu); dangerous surf and currents on the north shores — swim only in calm lagoons, and never on Sundays in village waters.
- **Rain**: 3–7.5 m a year; streams rise fast after storms; tropical cyclones (e.g. in 2004, 2005, 2018) and the 2009 tsunami have struck the islands.

### Relief

From the reef flats and sea to 966 m at Lata Mountain. Every island is the eroded remains of shield volcanoes: knife-edge ridges and amphitheatre valleys on Tutuila (Mount ʻAlava ridge at ~300–490 m, cliffs dropping straight into the north-shore bays), steep forested slopes and a narrow coastal flat on Ofu and Olosega, and on Taʻū a high summit plateau with craters cut off on the south by sea cliffs that rank among the highest in the world. In the game: ridges as narrow plateaus with ladder « stairs » (the Adventure Trail), bays as small beaches at the foot of cliffs, Pola Island as a tall 3D sea stack, the reef as a shallow turquoise shelf that drops to deep blue at its edge, Taʻū's south coast as a towering cliff wall with waterfalls.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: coastal strand and littoral forest (coconut, pandanus, beach heliotrope, Barringtonia) on the shores → lowland rain forest with huge banyans (aoa), Pometia (tava), Dysoxylum and tree ferns in the valleys (0–300 m) → montane rain forest on the ridges (300–600 m) → cloud forest, moss-hung and stunted, on Taʻū's summit plateau (600–966 m). Village gardens of taro, bananas, breadfruit (ʻulu) and coconuts fill the flats around the villages.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#1F7A3A` rain-forest green · `#7BC043` banana and taro leaf green · `#F4EDD8` white coral sand · `#3FC1C9` lagoon turquoise · `#0B5394` deep ocean blue beyond the reef · `#3B3B3B` dark basalt cliffs · `#C0392B` hibiscus and fale-mat red · `#E9D8A6` pandanus matting · `#6D4C41` fruit-bat brown.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): peʻa vao (Samoan flying fox, soaring by day on thermals) and peʻa fanua (white-naped flying fox, flying straight at dawn and dusk) — the park was created partly to protect them · sheath-tailed bat · honu (green turtle) and hawksbill · humpback whale (about August–October) · spinner dolphins · blacktip and whitetip reef sharks · giant clams (faisua) · parrotfish, butterflyfish, clownfish · seabirds on Pola Island: red-footed and brown boobies, frigatebirds, white-tailed tropicbirds (tavaʻe), noddies, white terns (manu sina) · forest birds: wattled honeyeater (iao), Samoan starling (fuia), many-coloured fruit dove (manuma), Pacific imperial pigeon (lupe), cardinal honeyeater · Pacific reef heron on the shores.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): banyan (aoa) · coconut (niu) · pandanus (fala, used for mats) · breadfruit (ʻulu) · taro (talo) · Tahitian chestnut (ifi) · the flowering Fagraea (pua) · tree ferns · hibiscus · beach morning glory · ʻava (kava pepper plant, for the ceremony).
- **Weather & seasons**: always warm (~24–31 °C) and humid; showers most days, heavier in the wet season (about October–May) with cyclone risk; the drier season (about June–September) has trade winds and calmer seas. Dense clouds cap Rainmaker Mountain and Taʻū's summit.
- **Night**: the Southern Cross and the Milky Way over Manuʻa's dark villages; flying foxes feeding in fruit trees; the evening **sā** (village prayer curfew) when villages fall quiet at dusk; geckos calling; the phosphorescent surf on the reef.
- **Sounds**: surf on the reef crest, rain on broad leaves, the squabbling of fruit bats in the trees, church bells and hymns on Sunday mornings, a village conch or bell for sā, log drums (lali), the slap of aiga-bus music on Tutuila, seabirds screaming around Pola Island.

## Moments

- **Flying foxes by day**: Samoan fruit bats soar over the ridges and valleys (Amalau, Vatia) in the middle of the day — one of the few places in the world to see this.
- **Palolo rising**: on a few nights after the October/November full moon, palolo reef worms swarm to the surface before dawn and villagers gather them with nets — a celebrated Samoan event.
- **Whales**: humpbacks pass and calve off the islands from about August to October.
- **Seabird evening at Pola Island**: boobies, frigatebirds and tropicbirds wheel back to the islet at dusk.
- **Sunday**: church in white, a quiet island, the family umu (earth-oven) lunch — and no swimming or hiking on some village land.
- **Coral spawning** on the reefs after spring full moons (around October–December), and low-tide reef walks.
- **Sunrise from Mount ʻAlava** over Pago Pago Harbor and the north coast.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a **768 × 640-tile** « vignette » map built from **three island insets** joined by a boat or small-plane hop: the Tutuila north coast (~60 % of the map: Fagasā Bay, the Mount ʻAlava ridge, Vatia bay with Pola Island, Amalau and Āfono, with Pago Pago's harbor edge and the visitor centre as the arrival hub at the south edge), Ofu Beach with the bridge to Olosega (~20 %), and Taʻū's south-east corner with Fitiʻuta, Saua and the cliff wall (~20 %). Near true scale: Vatia bay and Pola Island (~1 km), the Ofu beach and lagoon (~2 km → ~1 km), the Adventure Trail's ladders. Compressed ×3–5: the Mount ʻAlava ridge trail (5.6 km → ~1.5 km), the Taʻū coast trail. Cut: Tutuila's south side beyond the harbor, the interior of Taʻū (a cloud-forest backdrop).
- **Hubs & set pieces**: hubs at the Pago Pago visitor centre (a harbor-front town with aiga buses), Vatia village (a malae — village green — ringed by open fale), Ofu (ranger station and airstrip), Fitiʻuta. 3D landmarks: Pola Island, Mount ʻAlava with its tower and the rusty old tramway station, Rainmaker Mountain, Fatu Rock, the Blunts Point cannons, the Ofu–Olosega bridge, Taʻū's cliff wall with Laufuti Falls, the Lower Sauma star mound. Interiors: the visitor centre (models of the islands, fale, marine life), a traditional fale (open sides, woven mats, a guest must sit before speaking), a village church.
- **Activities**: **snorkelling** and reef exploration (Ofu's lagoon, Vatia bay in calm weather) with a fish-and-coral field guide; **hiking** the ridges with ladders; **wildlife**: fruit-bat watching at midday, seabirds at Pola, whales in season; **culture**: an ʻava ceremony (spill a few drops, say « manuia »), weaving a pandanus mat, making an umu, a siva (dance) evening; **ranger programmes**: guided hikes, reef walks, cultural demonstrations; **Young Ranger** ideas: tell the two flying foxes apart by silhouette, learn five Samoan words of greeting, count seabird species on Pola, plant a native tree on the ʻAlava trail (the park's invasive-tree removal), follow village etiquette to win a family's welcome; **water**: snorkel, swim in calm lagoons, paddle a paopao (outrigger canoe); **travel** between islands by small plane or boat.
- **Passport stamps**: the official cancellation list (2025) has two: « Pago Pago » and « Manua Islands ». Pago Pago's is at the National Park Visitor Center; the Manuʻa stamp at the Ofu ranger station (or with the Taʻū staff).
- **With friends / with the world**: the **village visit** as a party activity (the group sits in a fale, one player acts as the talking chief, everyone joins the ʻava ceremony); a **reef survey** where friends swim transects and pool their fish counts; the Adventure Trail ladders as a co-op climb; a canoe race in the Ofu lagoon; Sunday as a server-wide quiet day with church singing. Open-world gathering spots: the Vatia malae, the Pago Pago harbor front at sunset, Ofu Beach, the summit of Mount ʻAlava. Group photos: with Pola Island behind, on the Ofu–Olosega bridge.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay — and faʻasamoa): ask permission before using a beach, photographing people or hiking village land (small fees are normal); respect Sunday and the evening sā (stop and wait quietly); sit down before eating or talking in a fale, and don't eat while walking through a village; dress modestly in villages; never stand on or touch coral, never take shells or coral; keep away from seabird nests on Pola; don't disturb roosting fruit bats; watch for falling coconuts; pack out all trash.
- **Implementation challenges**: **three islands** far apart (insets and a travel hop); **coral reefs** and lagoons with an underwater layer for snorkelling; **surf and dangerous currents** on the north shores; **flying foxes** as large animated fliers by day; **cultural systems** (village permission, Sunday, sā, ʻava) that must be respectful and playable; **tropical rain and cyclones**; steep ridges with ladder stairs; very tall sea cliffs.
