# Pinnacles National Park — `PINN`

> California · established January 10, 2013 (Pinnacles National Monument since January 16, 1908) · 108.0 km²
> (26,685.73 acres) · 343,208 recreation visits (2025) · 251–1,007 m · NPS region PWR (DOI Region 10, California–Great
> Basin)
>
> The broken half of a 23-million-year-old volcano that the San Andreas Fault carried ~195 miles (314 km) north: a
> 2 km crown of rhyolite spires rising out of chaparral hills, with boulder-roofed gorges to crawl through and
> California condors riding the thermals overhead. In Hearthlight it should feel like a small, sun-baked playground of
> stone — headlamps in the dark caves, carved steps between the spires, and a three-metre shadow gliding across the
> path.

## Reading the map

The map is a bird's-eye perspective of an irregular park, ~13 km wide and 17 km tall, at the southern end of the Gabilan Mountains. Its core is the **High Peaks**: a 2 km north–south crown of spires and walls just west of centre. Everything else is organised by three creeks. The **West Fork of Chalone Creek** runs east along the north foot of the High Peaks through a narrow gorge roofed by fallen boulders — the **Balconies Cave**, between the Balconies cliffs and Machete Ridge — and out past the Old Pinnacles trailhead. **Bear Creek** drains the east face of the peaks down **Bear Gulch**: a reservoir, a talus cave and the Nature Center. Both empty into **Chalone Creek**, which runs south along the east side in a broad valley of oaks. South of Bear Gulch the **Chalone Peaks** ridge carries the park's high point (North Chalone Peak, 1,007 m, with a fire lookout). North of the peaks a single loop, the North Wilderness Trail, crosses roadless chaparral hills. Two thirds of the park is the Hain Wilderness.

**The east side.** CA 25 runs north–south along **Bear Valley**, just outside the east boundary. The East Entrance Road turns off it south-west past the East Entrance Station and the historic **Butterfield** and **Bacon** ranches to the **Park Store** (visitor centre) and camp store at the gate of **Pinnacles Campground**, the only place to sleep in the park (tents, RVs, tent cabins, a summer pool). The road then follows Chalone Creek south-west past the **Peaks View** parking area (spotting scopes trained on the High Peaks) and the Manzanita lot; the gravel Chalone Creek Road forks north to the **Old Pinnacles** trailhead; the main road climbs west up Bear Gulch to the **Bear Gulch Nature Center** (a stone CCC building) and day-use area, and ends at the tiny **Moses Spring** lot at the foot of the spires. From Bear Gulch, the Moses Spring Trail leads to **Bear Gulch Cave** and up through it to **Bear Gulch Reservoir**; the Condor Gulch and High Peaks trails climb north-west onto the peaks; the Chalone Peak Trail runs south from the reservoir.

**The west side.** CA 146 climbs from Soledad in the Salinas Valley to the **West Entrance** and the **West Pinnacles Visitor Contact Station** (the Prewett Point loop starts beside it). The Chaparral Road winds ~4 km north-east past the Jawbone lot to the **Chaparral Parking Area** (427 m), right under the west face of the peaks (Resurrection Wall, Pinnacle Rocks). From there the Juniper Canyon Trail switchbacks up to the High Peaks and the Balconies Trail runs north into the Balconies gorge and cave. The Chaparral lot and Bear Gulch are only ~2.5 km apart as the condor flies: a 1–2 hour drive round by King City, or a morning's walk over the top.

**The High Peaks crossing.** The trails make a figure of eight over the crown: from Bear Gulch up either Condor Gulch (an overlook at 1 mi) or the High Peaks Trail past Scout Peak; along the crest by the **Steep & Narrow** section — steps chiselled into the rock with metal handrails, hikers only — or by the parallel Tunnel Trail with its 1930s tunnel and bridge; then down Juniper Canyon to Chaparral. Hawkins Peak (829 m) and Scout Peak (794 m) are the named high points of the crown.

### Water

Small and seasonal. **Chalone Creek** flows south along the east side (the lowest point, 251 m, is where it leaves the park) and its **West Fork** runs east along the north foot of the peaks, *under* the boulders of the Balconies Cave; **Bear Creek** tumbles down Bear Gulch and runs through Bear Gulch Cave (a trickle and small cascades inside after rain); **Sandy Creek** crosses Bear Valley by the east gate. **Bear Gulch Reservoir**, a small CCC lake (~49,000 m³) behind a 13.7 m stone-faced dam, is the only standing water. **Moses Spring** seeps beside the trail. In winter and early spring the creeks run, the caves drip, and the Balconies Cave can flood deep enough to wade; by summer most creeks are dry beds of sycamore leaves. There is no swimming (except the campground pool, mid-April–September).

### Relief

From **251 m** (Chalone Creek at the south boundary) to **1,007 m** (North Chalone Peak). Rolling chaparral hills and ridges fill most of the park; the drama is concentrated in the **High Peaks**, a 2 km crown of rhyolite-breccia spires, fins and walls standing 150–300 m above Bear Gulch and Chaparral, and the **Balconies** cliffs (~200 m) facing Machete Ridge across the gorge. The **talus caves** are gorges so narrow that house-sized boulders fell in and jammed, making roofs: dark passages with low ceilings, steps, a creek and squeezes. The San Andreas Fault runs ~6 km east of the park (the Chalone Creek fault inside it is active; small quakes are frequent).

For the game: the spires are a cluster of **3D rock landmarks** (needles, fins, « the Fingers », « the Egg », Machete Ridge) rather than plateau cliffs; the Steep & Narrow is a run of **stairs** cut into a cliff with railings; the gulches are deep, narrow **banks-and-cliffs** canyons; the caves are **interiors** with boulders overhead. Camera note: looking north, the Balconies cliffs (south-facing) and the south faces of the spires read well; Bear Gulch, which runs east–west, shows its north wall in full.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: here slope and water matter more than height. Sun-baked slopes: chaparral of chamise, manzanita (red bark) and buckbrush, 82 % of the park. Cooler slopes and ridges: blue oak and gray pine (the only pine) woodland, California buckeye. Creek bottoms: valley oaks, western sycamores, Fremont cottonwoods (Bench and South Wilderness trails). Meadows of wild oats and native bunchgrass. On the High Peaks: bare breccia painted with chartreuse and orange lichens; ferns, mosses and stinging nettle at the cave mouths.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#C8955A` breccia ochre (spire faces in daylight) · `#D9773A` spires at sunset · `#B7C23C` chartreuse lichen · `#6F7A3E` chamise olive · `#8E4A36` manzanita bark · `#D6B86A` summer grass · `#9CC45A` spring grass · `#7F9C8C` gray-pine blue-green · `#2B2723` cave dark · `#4E7D6C` reservoir water.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): **California condor** (year-round; High Peaks roosts at dawn and dusk, soaring on midday thermals, also the ridge south-east of the campground — each bird wears a numbered wing tag, 117 wild in the central California flock at the end of 2025); turkey vulture (V-shaped wobbling flight, the look-alike); **prairie falcon** (~11 pairs, the densest nesting known — cliffs, January–August), peregrine falcon, golden eagle; **Townsend's big-eared bat** (Bear Gulch Cave, summer colony) and western mastiff bat (Balconies); **California red-legged frog** (Bear Gulch Reservoir at night, reintroduced 2001–04); western pond turtle (rare); western fence lizard and coast horned lizard (sunny rocks); **tarantula** (males wander September–October); acorn woodpecker (granaries in gray pines by the visitor centres); California quail; canyon wren; bobcat, gray fox, black-tailed deer; western rattlesnake; convergent ladybugs in winter clusters; ~450–500 species of **native bees**.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): manzanita (bells January–February), shooting star and Indian warrior (January–February), buckbrush (white, March), California poppy and bush poppy (March), larkspur, chia and bush lupine (April), chamise (white spikes May–July), clarkias and penstemons (May), blue oak, valley oak, gray pine (pollen March–April), California buckeye (leaves in February, bare by July, nuts until November), and poison oak everywhere (red in spring) — the plant to *recognise*.
- **Weather & seasons**: winter is green and quiet, with rain, running creeks, flooded Balconies Cave and frost; late winter–spring (February–May) is the high season of flowers, condors and crowds; summer is fierce heat (hike at dawn, 1 L of water per hour), dry creeks and very high fire danger; autumn brings cooler days, tarantulas and golden grass.
- **Night**: dark skies with the Milky Way over the spires (occasional ranger night-sky programmes and star parties at the campground and the east visitor centre); owls (great horned by the reservoir, western screech-owls at Bear Gulch); bats leaving the caves at dusk; tarantulas on the roads in autumn; frogs on rainy nights; moonrise behind the High Peaks.
- **Sounds**: the descending trill of the canyon wren echoing off the spires, the whoosh of a condor's wings, prairie falcons' « kek-kek-kek », California quail, acorn woodpeckers' « waka-waka », the hum of bees on spring flowers, dripping water and hollow echoes in the caves, climbers calling « on belay », a rattlesnake's buzz, hot-wind hiss in the chaparral.

## Moments

- **Condors on the High Peaks**: all year; best at dawn (birds sunning on rocks and gray pines before the thermals) and in the evening (returning to roost); scopes at Peaks View and on the Bench Trail. Courtship in winter, one egg January–March, chicks fledge at ~6 months. Four juveniles were released at Pinnacles in October 2025.
- **The spring bloom**: January–July, peak March–May, when more than 80 % of the plants flower — manzanita first, then poppies and buckbrush, then chia, lupine, clarkias and chamise.
- **Bear Gulch Cave fully open**: the upper cave opens only briefly, usually the last week of March and the last week of October; the whole cave closes mid-May to mid-July while the bats raise their pups; the lower cave is open the rest of the year.
- **Tarantula season**: September–October, males roam day and night looking for mates.
- **Raptor nesting**: January–August; climbing advisories on the nest cliffs January–July.
- **Winter flow**: after storms (January–March) the creeks run, small waterfalls appear inside the caves, and the Balconies Cave floods.
- **Ladybug swarms**: clusters overwinter in Bear Gulch and along the Old Pinnacles trail and scatter on the first warm spring days.
- **Sunset at Prewett Point** and **moonrise behind the High Peaks** from Chalone Creek.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: ~768 × 576 tiles. Keep the 7.5 × 3 km core — West Contact Station → Chaparral → High Peaks → Bear Gulch → Peaks View → campground — compressed ~1:5 east–west, with the High Peaks crossing at ~1:3 and the two caves near true scale (Bear Gulch Cave's ~300 m passage as ~150–200 tiles of interior). Squeeze the Chalone Peaks ridge into a short spur south of the reservoir and the North Wilderness into one loop of hills; cut the far south. The west and east road networks must stay **disconnected**: the only link is on foot.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: the Park Store and campground (east), the Bear Gulch Nature Center (a stone CCC building, interior with the seismograph drum) and the West Contact Station; 3D landmarks: the High Peaks spires, Machete Ridge, the Balconies cliffs, the North Chalone fire lookout, the Bear Gulch dam; interiors: Bear Gulch Cave (lower and upper), Balconies Cave, the Tunnel Trail tunnel.
- **Activities**: hiking the figure-eight of loops; photo spots (Condor Gulch Overlook, the Steep & Narrow, Balconies Cliffs, the reservoir, Prewett Point at sunset, the Chalone lookout, the Bacon Ranch barn at night); a **condor field guide by wing tag** (tag colour + number = a named individual; spot them all over the seasons); bat-watching from outside the cave at dusk; cave exploring with a headlamp (light is a resource); easy climbing on the Reservoir and Discovery Wall crags; bee and wildflower herbarium; star parties; Junior Ranger (the booklet's three levels — acorn woodpecker 3–6, coyote 7–10, condor 11+ — and its « Condor Comeback » activity); vehicles: car, bike on the service roads, the weekend shuttle.
- **Passport stamps**: the official list has a single cancellation, « Pinnacles NP — Paicines » (at the Park Store / visitor centre by the campground); stamp collectors also report stamps at the Bear Gulch Nature Center and the West Pinnacles Visitor Contact Station, plus bonus stamps (Junior Ranger badge, the 1908–2008 centennial).
- **With friends / with the world**: start the party split — half at the east gate, half at the west — and meet on the High Peaks (the map's natural rendezvous, since no road joins the sides); single-file squeeze through the caves with one shared headlamp; a condor count where each player logs different tags; a climbing rope team on the Reservoir crags; the campground amphitheatre star party as the open-world evening gathering; a group photo on the Steep & Narrow railing.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): enter only open cave sections and never shine lights on bats; stay on trail (poison oak, fragile chaparral); never approach or feed condors (and learn why lead ammunition kills them); keep food from the campground raccoons; no pets on trails; carry water in the heat (a hydration meter in summer); respect raptor closures on the climbing cliffs; no drones.
- **Implementation challenges**: walk-through talus caves with two entrances, low ceilings, boulders overhead, a creek and seasonal flooding (the saga's dark-cave dungeon interiors are the nearest tool, but these must be seamless passages, not instances); needle-like spires that the plateau/cliff system can't make (3D rock props); carved stairs with railings; soaring condor flight on thermals (circling AI, roosts at dawn/dusk); the two disconnected road networks; summer heat as a mechanic; seasonal cave closures on a calendar.
