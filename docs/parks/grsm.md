# Great Smoky Mountains National Park — `GRSM`

> Tennessee & North Carolina · established June 15, 1934 · 2,114.2 km² (522,427 acres) · 11,527,939 recreation visits (2025) ·
> 267–2,025 m · NPS region SER (today DOI Region 2, South Atlantic–Gulf)
>
> The most visited national park in the country: a 90 km wall of forested ridges along the Tennessee–North Carolina line, fading
> into blue haze, with log-cabin valleys, cold creeks and the highest peaks of the southern Appalachians. In Hearthlight it should
> feel like a green, rainy, humming place — cabins and fields in the coves, fireflies at night, bears in the trees, and always one
> more ridge behind the last one.

## Reading the map

North up, the park is a long, slightly bent band tilted from the west-south-west to the east-north-east: its narrow western tip sits on the Little Tennessee River lakes (Chilhowee, Calderwood, Cheoah) near US 129, its broad eastern end on the Pigeon River gorge where I-40 crosses into North Carolina. Down its whole length runs the main crest, which is also the state line and the Appalachian Trail (green dashes): from Fontana Dam in the south-west it climbs to Gregory Bald, Spence Field and Thunderhead, runs east over Silers Bald to Kuwohi (the highest point, in the middle of the band), dips to Newfound Gap, then runs north-east over Charlies Bunion and Mount Guyot to Mount Cammerer and down to Davenport Gap by I-40. Everything north of the crest is Tennessee — short, steep valleys that drop to Townsend, Gatlinburg and Cosby. Everything south is North Carolina — longer ridges and creeks that run south into Fontana Lake (the south-west boundary) or to the Oconaluftee valley at Cherokee.

Only one road crosses the park: **Newfound Gap Road (US 441)**, the red spine in the middle of the map. It leaves Gatlinburg south, passes the **Sugarlands Visitor Center** (park headquarters) 3 km inside the park, climbs 25 km up the West Prong of the Little Pigeon River past the Chimneys picnic area and the Loop (where the road spirals over itself below Chimney Tops), tops out at **Newfound Gap** (1,538 m) on the state line, then winds 28 km down the Oconaluftee River past Smokemont and the **Oconaluftee Visitor Center** into Cherokee. From just below the gap, **Kuwohi Road** runs 11 km south-west along the crest to the **Kuwohi Visitor Center** and the tower on the summit (closed in winter). From Sugarlands, **Little River Road** heads west 28 km along the Little River — the Elkmont turn-off, the Sinks, Metcalf Bottoms — to the Townsend "Wye", where **Laurel Creek Road** continues 12 km south-west into **Cades Cove**, a flat open valley in the west third of the park ringed by the 11-mile (17.7 km) one-way **Cades Cove Loop Road** with the **Cades Cove Visitor Center** and Cable Mill at its far (south-west) corner. Two gravel roads leave the cove: Rich Mountain Road one-way north to Townsend, and Parson Branch Road one-way south-west to US 129.

The rest of the network is dead-end spurs poking in from the edges, each a small district of its own. Along the north edge, from west to east: the **Foothills Parkway** (a scenic parkway run by the park, just outside its boundary) with **Look Rock** tower and **Abrams Creek**; **Townsend**; **Gatlinburg** with the one-way **Roaring Fork Motor Nature Trail** looping through the forest under Mount Le Conte; **Greenbrier** up the Middle Prong; **Cosby**; and **Big Creek** at I-40 exit 451. Down the east side: gravel Mount Sterling Road from Big Creek and **Cove Creek Road** from I-40 exit 20 both reach **Cataloochee**, a remote high valley of farm buildings and elk; south of it **Balsam Mountain** and Heintooga are reached from the Blue Ridge Parkway, which starts at Oconaluftee and climbs east across the Cherokee Indian Reservation (Qualla Boundary, the tan wedge that bites into the park's south-east). Along the south: **Deep Creek** and **Lakeview Drive** (the "Road to Nowhere", which ends at a tunnel) out of Bryson City, then Fontana Dam and **Twentymile** in the south-west corner.

The big roadless block is the south-western quarter between Kuwohi and Fontana Lake: Noland, Forney, Hazel and Eagle creeks run south in parallel valleys to the lake, reached only by long trails or by boat across the lake (Hazel Creek's ghost lumber town, Proctor). The north-eastern block between Greenbrier, Cosby and Cataloochee (Mount Guyot, Mount Sterling, Balsam Mountain ridge) is the other wild core. Relief organises everything: hubs sit in the valleys at 300–800 m, the crest stays at 1,500–2,000 m, and every trail on the trail map is a climb from one to the other.

### Water

No natural lakes: the Smokies are a land of fast, clear streams (about 2,900 miles / 4,700 km of them) fed by very high rainfall, with rhododendron tunnels along the banks and big grey boulders in the beds. On the Tennessee side the streams run north: the **West Prong of the Little Pigeon River** along Newfound Gap Road down through Sugarlands to Gatlinburg; the **Middle Prong** through Greenbrier (Ramsey Cascades); the **Little River** from the crest west past Elkmont, the Sinks and Metcalf Bottoms to the Wye and Townsend, joined there by its Middle Prong from Tremont; **Abrams Creek** drains Cades Cove west through a gorge (Abrams Falls) to Chilhowee Lake; **Cosby Creek** and **Big Creek** run north-east to the Pigeon River. On the North Carolina side they run south: the **Oconaluftee River** (with Bradley Fork and Raven Fork) from Newfound Gap past Smokemont and Oconaluftee to Cherokee; **Deep Creek** to Bryson City; **Noland, Forney, Hazel and Eagle creeks** into **Fontana Lake**, a long, many-armed TVA reservoir that forms the south-west boundary (its level drops several metres in winter, exposing red-clay banks). **Cataloochee Creek** runs north-east to the Pigeon. Around 80 named waterfalls, mostly cascades of 5–30 m. Streams swell fast in storms; swimming holes (the Wye, the Sinks, Midnight Hole, Deep Creek) are real summer gathering spots.

### Relief

From 267 m at the mouth of Abrams Creek to 2,025 m on Kuwohi; 16 peaks top 1,830 m (6,000 ft). The land is one great crest with long spur ridges branching off it at right angles like the ribs of a fish: Sugarland Mountain, Bote and Rich mountains, Balsam Mountain and Mount Sterling Ridge, Thomas Divide, Noland Divide, Forney Ridge, Welch Ridge. The Tennessee face is short and steep (Le Conte rises 1,600 m straight out of Gatlinburg); the North Carolina side slopes more gradually. Between ridges lie **coves**: Cades Cove (a flat limestone "window" at ~530 m, about 7 × 3 km) and Cataloochee (~790 m) are the big open ones. Rock is old grey sandstone and dark slate (the Anakeesta slate breaks into the sharp pinnacles of Chimney Tops, Charlies Bunion and Alum Cave Bluffs); limestone under Cades Cove and Whiteoak Sink holds caves (closed to visitors). In the game: the crest is a long high plateau band with 3D peaks (Kuwohi with its tower, Le Conte's three summits, Guyot, Chimney Tops' twin spires, Charlies Bunion's knife-edge); balds are smooth grassy domes made of one-level banks; coves are flat meadows boxed in by steep forest slopes; trails climb with stone stairs (Alum Cave, Chimney Tops); cliffs only at the slate pinnacles, bluffs, Charlies Bunion's north face and the Jumpoff (a sheer drop off Mount Kephart's north side, 1,869 m).

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation**: below ~1,000 m, **cove hardwood forest** (tulip poplar, basswood, yellow buckeye, sugar maple, eastern hemlock along creeks) with dense **rosebay rhododendron and dog-hobble** thickets on the banks; dry south-facing ridges carry **pine-oak** woods. 1,000–1,500 m: **northern hardwoods** (yellow birch, American beech, "beech gaps" in the saddles). Above ~1,500 m: **spruce-fir forest** of red spruce and Fraser fir — the fir largely killed by the balsam woolly adelgid, so the summits bristle with silver snags over young green firs. Scattered on the crest: **grassy balds** (Gregory, Andrews, Spence Field) and **heath balds** ("laurel hells") of catawba rhododendron and mountain laurel.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#7C93B8` blue-haze near ridges · `#B6C3D8` far ridges fading into sky · `#3F6B3D` cove forest canopy · `#23402F` spruce-fir · `#A8BE78` grassy bald & Cades Cove meadow · `#C9B868` late-summer hayfield · `#9A9C92` lichen-grey sandstone boulder · `#3D4045` wet Anakeesta slate · `#4F7F86` creek pool · `#7A5A3C` weathered chestnut logs of the cabins.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): **black bear** (~1,900 in the park, the densest population in the country; Cades Cove edges at dusk, May–August) · **elk** (Oconaluftee fields and Cataloochee at dawn and dusk; rut September–October) · **white-tailed deer** and **wild turkey** (Cades Cove meadows) · **synchronous firefly** (Elkmont, late May–June nights) · **blue ghost firefly** (low glowing drifters on the forest floor, May) · **red-cheeked salamander** (found nowhere but these mountains; damp high-elevation logs, rainy nights) · **hellbender** (giant salamander under river rocks, Little River) · **southern Appalachian brook trout** (cold high streams) · **river otter** (Abrams Creek, reintroduced) · **groundhog** (roadsides in the cove) · **Carolina northern flying squirrel** (spruce-fir, night) · **peregrine falcon** (cliffs near Alum Cave, spring) · **common raven** and **dark-eyed junco** (on the crest, Kuwohi parking) · **barred owl** (calls in the coves at night) · **timber rattlesnake** (sunny rocks, give it room).
- **Flora** (the herbarium): large-flowered trillium (April) · dwarf crested iris (April) · fire pink (April–May) · mountain laurel (May–June) · flame azalea (June, Gregory Bald) · catawba rhododendron (June, high balds) · rosebay rhododendron (July, creek sides) · Turk's cap lily (July–August, high meadows) · tulip poplar (giant cove trees, gold in October) · Fraser fir (only in these southern crests; the Christmas-tree smell of the summit).
- **Weather & seasons**: spring comes up the mountains from March (valleys) to May (crest), with rain and flowers; summer is hot and humid with afternoon thunderstorms most days and the haze that names the range; autumn colour starts on the crest in early October and reaches the valleys by early November; winter is mild in the coves, but the crest gets snow and rime ice (feathers of frost on every twig), closing Newfound Gap Road for hours or days. Fog fills Cades Cove on cool mornings; clouds cap Kuwohi most days. Fire: the 2016 Chimney Tops 2 fire burned from the Chimneys into Gatlinburg; Hurricane Helene (2024) washed out roads on the North Carolina side.
- **Night**: fireflies are the star (synchronous at Elkmont, blue ghosts elsewhere); foxfire (glowing fungus) on rotten logs; barred owls and whip-poor-wills; bats over the creeks; the sky is dark enough for the Milky Way from Cataloochee, Balsam Mountain and Look Rock, but Gatlinburg and Pigeon Forge glow orange behind the northern ridges.
- **Sounds**: rushing water everywhere, the tick of rain on rhododendron leaves, wood thrush flutes at dusk, elk bugles echoing in Cataloochee in autumn, wind in the firs at Kuwohi, the creak and splash of the Cable Mill wheel and the hum of Mingus Mill's millstones, a church bell in Cades Cove, fiddle and banjo on a visitor-centre porch, the crunch of gravel on the one-way roads.

## Moments

- **Synchronous fireflies at Elkmont**: *Photinus carolinus* males flash 5–8 times together, then the whole forest goes dark for about 8 seconds, then again; the display lasts about two to three weeks between late May and mid-June (2026 window: May 20–27). Viewing is by lottery (applications in late April; 120 cars a night, 960 passes in 2026), after dark (roughly 9–11 pm); red lights only, stay on the trail.
- **Elk rut**: early September to late October, bulls bugle and spar at dawn and dusk in the Cataloochee fields and beside the Oconaluftee Visitor Center; calves are hidden in the grass in late May–June.
- **Spring wildflowers**: mid-March to late May, peaking in April at low and mid elevations (Porters Creek, Chestnut Top, Little River); the Spring Wildflower Pilgrimage walks happen in late April.
- **June blooms on the balds**: flame azaleas turn Gregory Bald orange, red and yellow in mid-to-late June; catawba rhododendron flowers purple-pink on the high crest and Andrews Bald in June.
- **Autumn colour**: the wave runs downhill — high spruce-fir ridges and yellow birches in early October, the peak at middle and low elevations from mid-October to early November; also the busiest weeks of the year.
- **Sunrise and sunset from the heights**: sunrise at Myrtle Point on Le Conte, sunset at Cliff Tops (lodge guests' ritual), both ends of the day from the Kuwohi tower; morning mist lying in Cades Cove seen from the loop.
- **Mountain Life Festival** (a Saturday in mid-September, Mountain Farm Museum): sorghum pressed by a mule-turned mill and boiled into molasses, apple butter, hominy, hearth cooking, old-time music.
- **Vehicle-free Wednesdays in Cades Cove** (May–September): the loop belongs to walkers and cyclists.
- **Llama day**: in season, a string of llamas carries food and linen up the Trillium Gap Trail past Grotto Falls to LeConte Lodge, a few times a week.
- **Winter on the crest**: rime ice and snow on Kuwohi and Newfound Gap while the valleys stay green; Kuwohi Road is closed December–March and becomes a snowshoe route; Rainbow Falls can freeze into a column.

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a **2048 × 1024-tile** map (the real band is ~90 × 35 km, so the whole park is compressed ~30× on average, keeping its long WSW–ENE shape and the crest as its backbone). Near true scale (1 tile ≈ 1.5 m): the Cable Mill and VC complex (~150 m across), the churches and the John Oliver Cabin, Newfound Gap's parking terrace and memorial, the Kuwohi summit with its 14 m tower and ~800 m ramp-trail, Elkmont's clubhouse row, the Mountain Farm Museum, LeConte Lodge and its summit knobs, Grotto Falls. Moderately compressed (×5–10): Cades Cove itself (a 17.7 km loop becomes a ~2–3 km loop, ~1,500 tiles around, keeping its oval meadow and the two cross lanes), Cataloochee's valley road, the Roaring Fork loop. Heavily compressed (×40+): the forest between hubs, the long North Carolina creek valleys, the Foothills Parkway, the Fontana shore. Cut or reduced to a boat stop: Twentymile, Abrams Creek campground, most of the Lakeshore Trail.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: gathering hubs at **Sugarlands** (visitor centre interior, parking lot, stable), **Cades Cove** (store, campground, Cable Mill), **Newfound Gap** (state-line marker and the stone memorial terrace), **Oconaluftee** (visitor centre with elk in the field, farm museum), **Elkmont** (campground and clubhouse). 3D landmarks: the Kuwohi tower (a concrete mushroom on a long spiral ramp), Chimney Tops' twin spires, Le Conte's bulk, Charlies Bunion, Mount Cammerer's octagonal stone lookout, Look Rock tower, Fontana Dam, the Loop's road spiral with its tunnel, the Lakeview Drive tunnel, Cable Mill's waterwheel, Mingus Mill's flume. Interiors: Sugarlands and Oconaluftee visitor centres, Cable Mill (millstones), Mingus Mill (turbine and hopper), a Cades Cove church (pews, pulpit, bell rope), Little Greenbrier School (slate boards, desks), a LeConte Lodge cabin (kerosene lamp, wool blankets), the Appalachian Clubhouse's great room.
- **Activities**: hiking the classic climbs (Alum Cave to Le Conte, Rainbow Falls, Charlies Bunion, Ramsey Cascades); photo spots (Kuwohi tower view, Newfound Gap state-line sign, the Loop, Chimney Tops from the overlook, Cable Mill wheel, John Oliver Cabin in the fog, Palmer Chapel with elk, Grotto Falls from behind, Andrews Bald, Cliff Tops at sunset, the Lakeview tunnel); wildlife spotting on the Cades Cove loop at dusk (bears in apple trees, turkeys, deer) and elk in Cataloochee; a salamander night walk with a ranger (turn no rocks — look with the light); fly-fishing for brook trout; tubing and swimming (the Wye, the Sinks, Deep Creek, Midnight Hole); cycling the loop on car-free Wednesdays; horseback and hayrides from the Cades Cove, Smokemont and Sugarlands stables; the llama train to the lodge; milling cornmeal at Cable and Mingus mills, pressing sorghum at the festival; boat across Fontana Lake to Hazel Creek and ghostly Proctor; snowshoeing Kuwohi Road in winter. Junior ranger ideas: count the flashes and dark seconds of the fireflies, match a cabin's logs to its notches, find the three churches' bells, identify five salamanders by colour, deliver a lodge supply pack with the llamas, learn the Cherokee names of places (Kuwohi = "mulberry place").
- **Passport stamps**: Sugarlands VC; Oconaluftee VC (also "Trail of Tears" and "Blue Ridge Parkway" stamps); Cades Cove VC (and the campground kiosk); Kuwohi VC (plus an Appalachian Trail stamp); Gatlinburg Welcome Center; Townsend Visitors Center; the Swain County Visitor Center in Bryson City; the GSM Institute at Tremont; the Deep Creek and Cataloochee ranger stations (reported by collectors, not an official NPS list).
- **With friends / with the world**: the Cades Cove loop is the natural "MMO road" — everyone drives, bikes or walks one way round it, so strangers meet at every stop and at "bear jams" where a bear is sighted; the Newfound Gap terrace (a group photo straddling the state line, one foot in each state), the Kuwohi tower ramp at sunset, and the Elkmont firefly trail (dark, whispered, only red lights) are the natural gatherings. Co-op ideas: a party books the firefly lottery together and must reach the trail by dusk; relay the llama string up the Trillium Gap Trail; a four-player hayride; one player holds the light while others spot salamanders; synchronised flashing emote for a group firefly photo.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): stay 50 yards (46 m) from bears and don't let one get your food (a fed bear gets relocated — a "bear score" drops if players leave food out); keep well back from elk (the park bans approaching within 25 yards or at any distance that disturbs them); red light only and no leaving the trail at the firefly viewing (females live on the ground); don't move rocks or stack them in creeks (salamander homes); no wading above waterfalls; park only with a parking tag, never on the grass of the cove; pack out everything; pick no flowers.
- **Implementation challenges**: the **haze** — a layered blue aerial-perspective that fades each ridge more than the last is the park's signature and must work at low resolution; very dense, varied forest (cove hardwoods vs spruce-fir with dead grey snags vs rhododendron tunnels); **fireflies** synchronised across a whole valley (thousands of lights, a shared clock); the Loop road that passes under itself (bridge + tunnel in a tile world); a road network that is mostly one-way (Cades Cove, Roaring Fork, Rich Mountain, Parson Branch, Balsam Mountain); balds as smooth open domes on a steep crest; rime ice and winter snow on the crest only (elevation-driven weather); Fontana Lake's seasonal drawdown; herds of elk that react to player distance; the busy car traffic of the real park (bear jams) as a cozy mechanic rather than a nuisance.
