# Gates of the Arctic National Park & Preserve — `GAAR`

> Alaska · established December 2, 1980 (national monument since December 1, 1978) · 30,448.2 km² (7,523,897 acres) of park, ~34,290 km² (8.47 million acres) with the preserve · 14,923 recreation visits (2025) ·
> ~120–2,594 m · NPS region AKR (Alaska)
>
> The northernmost US national park: the central Brooks Range above the Arctic Circle, with no roads, no trails and no facilities, crossed by six wild rivers and by half a million migrating caribou, home of the Nunamiut of Anaktuvuk Pass. In Hearthlight it should feel like pure wilderness made gentle: step off a bush plane onto a gravel bar, walk tussock tundra under a sun that never sets, float a clear river between two mountains called « the Gates », and watch the caribou pour through a pass.

## Reading the map

The map is wider than tall, north up. The park is a big, irregular green block in the middle of the sheet, spanning the **Brooks Range** from west to east: the white-grey, shaded **Endicott Mountains** in the east-centre and the **Schwatka Mountains** in the south-west. A red dotted **Continental Divide** wanders along the range's crest: rivers north of it drain to the Arctic Ocean (the Killik, Chandler, Anaktuvuk, Itkillik, north to the Colville on the « North Slope »), rivers south of it to the Yukon via the Koyukuk (the Alatna, John, North Fork Koyukuk, Tinayguk) or to Kotzebue Sound via the Kobuk and Noatak. Two lighter **preserve** units sit at the north-east corner (upper Itkillik and Nanushuk country) and the south-west corner (the Kobuk valley below the Angayucham Mountains); most of the park is labelled **National Park Wilderness**. A small detached square of park wilderness sits north of the range around Castle Mountain and Fortress Mountain.

The **east edge** is the travel corridor: the red **Dalton Highway** (route 11) and the pipeline run north from **Prospect Creek** and the Arctic Circle past the Jack White Range, **Coldfoot** (« Arctic Interagency Visitor Center », airstrip), Marion Creek Campground and **Wiseman**, up the Dietrich River past Sukakpak Mountain to **Atigun Pass** (4,739 ft) and the North Slope (Galbraith Lake Campground). The park boundary runs a few miles west of the road. In the south, **Bettles/Evansville** (ranger station, airstrip) sits on the Koyukuk River below the park, linked in winter only by an ice road; Alatna and Allakaket lie on the Arctic Circle to the south-west; the Iñupiat villages of Kobuk and Shungnak are off the south-west corner.

Inside the park, the map names the classic places: **Anaktuvuk Pass** (2,200 ft / 671 m) — the Nunamiut village with a ranger station and airstrip — in a wide pass on the divide, with the John River flowing south and the Anaktuvuk River north; the **Gates of the Arctic** themselves, **Frigid Crags** (5,501 ft) and **Boreal Mountain** (6,654 ft) on either side of the **North Fork Koyukuk**, with Mount Doonerak (7,457 ft) just north-east; the **Arrigetch Peaks** (7,190 ft) and **Mount Igikpak** (8,510 ft / 2,594 m on the map, the range's highest here) at the head of the Alatna and Kobuk; the long **Walker Lake** at the headwaters of the Kobuk; lakes like Chandler, Kurupa, Kipmik, Isiak, Matcharak, Takahula, Circle, Summit and Wild lakes; Oolah Pass and Ernie Pass in the east; Howard Pass to the Noatak in the far west. Scale: 0–20 km / 0–20 mi. A note bottom-right warns that private (Native) lands lie inside the boundaries: respect landowners and contact the park for easements.

Nothing on the map is a trail: the park has **no roads, trails or campgrounds** at all — only rivers, passes and valleys to follow. The only « roads » in the data are the few village streets of Anaktuvuk Pass.

### Water

The **Continental Divide** splits the park: the Anaktuvuk, Itkillik, Killik and Chandler rivers flow north to the Colville and the Arctic Ocean; the Alatna, John, North Fork Koyukuk and Tinayguk flow south to the Koyukuk and the Yukon; the Kobuk and Noatak flow west to Kotzebue Sound. Six are designated **Wild and Scenic Rivers** (Alatna, John, Kobuk, Noatak, North Fork Koyukuk, Tinayguk). They are mostly clear, cold and braided, flowing over wide gravel bars that double as airstrips; ice forms from October and break-up comes in late May–June, with aufeis (layered overflow ice) lingering in some valleys into summer. Lakes fill glacial basins: **Walker Lake** (14 miles long, at the head of the Kobuk), Chandler, Kurupa, Kipmik, Isiak, Matcharak, Takahula, Circle and Summit lakes — many are float-plane landing strips. Permafrost underlies most of the land, making spongy, wet tussock tundra even on slopes. Reed Hot Springs bubbles up in the south-west.

### Relief

From ~120 m in the south-western Kobuk lowlands to **Mount Igikpak, 2,594 m (8,510 ft)** on the park map (newer surveys give ~2,520 m). The Brooks Range here is a sea of sharp grey peaks of 1,500–2,300 m cut by deep, U-shaped glacial valleys with flat, braided floors at 300–700 m; small cirque glaciers cling to the highest peaks; the **Arrigetch** are granite spires and walls rising ~1,500 m above their valleys; the northern ranges are pale limestone. Passes are low and broad (Anaktuvuk Pass 671 m). South of the range the land drops to forested hills and river plains; north, it rolls into the tundra foothills of the North Slope. In the game: long, flat valley floors with braided channels between steep slope tiles and cliff bands; 3D peaks for Frigid Crags and Boreal Mountain (a gateway pair across the river), Mount Doonerak and a cluster of spiky Arrigetch spires; tussock ground that slows walking; gravel bars as landing strips.

## Look & feel

- **Vegetation by elevation (and latitude)**: south-facing valleys below ~700 m hold the **northern tree line**: black and white spruce, paper birch, balsam poplar, willow and alder thickets along rivers; above and north of it, **tussock tundra** (cottongrass tussocks, dwarf birch, Labrador tea, blueberry, lichens — « reindeer moss »), then dry alpine tundra and fellfield with mountain avens, moss campion and Arctic poppies; bare scree and small glaciers on the peaks. Walker Lake marks the northern limit of forest.
- **Ground, rock & water colours**: `#1F3A2C` black spruce · `#8FA35F` summer tundra · `#C04A2B` autumn bearberry red · `#E0B53C` autumn willow and birch gold · `#EDE7D2` cottongrass seed heads · `#D9DDC4` reindeer lichen · `#7A7D80` grey limestone and shale peaks · `#B9A98E` granite of the Arrigetch · `#BFC4C1` braided-river gravel · `#6FA8B8` clear river water.
- **Wildlife** (the field guide): **caribou** (Western Arctic, Teshekpuk, Central Arctic and Porcupine herds — over 500,000 animals — migrate through the central Brooks Range north in spring, south in late summer–autumn), grizzly bear, black bear (south), gray wolf, wolverine, Dall sheep (cliffs above the valleys), moose (willow flats), muskox (rare, north), lynx, red and Arctic foxes, Arctic ground squirrel, hoary marmot, collared pika, snowshoe hare; birds from four continents — gyrfalcon, golden eagle, rock and willow ptarmigan, northern wheatear (winters in Africa), Arctic warbler (Asia), long-tailed jaeger, Lapland longspur, Arctic tern; fish — Arctic grayling, lake trout, Arctic char, sheefish and chum salmon in the Kobuk.
- **Flora** (the herbarium): cottongrass (white tufts, July), mountain avens (Dryas, June), Arctic poppy (June–July), moss campion (June), Labrador tea (June), bearberry (red leaves, late August), cloudberry and blueberry (August), dwarf fireweed on gravel bars, reindeer lichen, the northernmost spruce.
- **Weather & seasons**: late May–June break-up and ice on the lakes, snow in the passes; July warm, buggy (mosquitoes!), thunderstorms and wildfire smoke some years; mid-August the tundra blazes red and gold, first snow on the peaks, the caribou come south; September–May long, dark, bitter winter (−40 to −50 °C) with only dog teams, snowmachines and ski-planes.
- **Night**: 24-hour daylight — at Anaktuvuk Pass the sun doesn't set from about May 27 to July 18 (and it doesn't rise for about four weeks around the winter solstice, longer behind the mountains); Bettles has midnight sun from early June to about July 10; the aurora returns in late August and Coldfoot and Wiseman are famous winter aurora spots; very dark, clean skies.
- **Sounds**: wind in the tussocks, a river rushing over gravel, the click of caribou tendons as a herd walks past, wolves howling, ptarmigan calls, a single-engine plane landing on a gravel bar, mosquitoes, the crack of lake ice at break-up, and total silence.

## Moments

- **The caribou migration** through Anaktuvuk Pass and the valleys: northbound in spring (April–June), southbound from mid-August to October; the Nunamiut still hunt them, as people have for 11,000 years.
- **Midnight sun**: at Anaktuvuk Pass no sunset from about May 27 to July 18; hiking at midnight in golden light.
- **Polar night**: about four weeks without sunrise at Anaktuvuk Pass around the winter solstice (longer behind the mountains); pink noon twilight.
- **Autumn colour** (~August 15–September 5): red bearberry and gold willows across the tundra, fresh snow on the peaks.
- **Aurora season** (late August–April): the northern lights over Wiseman and Coldfoot, among the best places in Alaska.
- **Break-up** (late May–June): river ice and aufeis breaking up, floatplanes returning to the lakes.
- **Floating through the Gates**: passing between Frigid Crags and Boreal Mountain on the North Fork Koyukuk, as Bob Marshall named them in 1929.
- **Crossing the Arctic Circle** on the Dalton Highway (a sign and photo spot south of Coldfoot).

## For the game

- **Scale & shape**: a curated map of **~1,024 × 768 tiles** built as linked vignettes, all at near-true scale locally with the wilderness between them cut: (1) **Anaktuvuk Pass** ~384 × 288 tiles — the village streets, airstrip, ranger station and museum in a broad tundra pass with the John and Anaktuvuk valleys opening south and north, and caribou herds streaming through; (2) **The Gates** ~384 × 256 tiles — a stretch of the North Fork Koyukuk (~8 km compressed ×3) with gravel-bar landings between the two portal peaks, for a float and a camp; (3) **Arrigetch valley** ~256 × 256 tiles — Arrigetch Creek climbing from the Alatna to a cirque ringed by granite spires; (4) a **Coldfoot–Wiseman** gateway strip (~256 × 160) on the Dalton Highway with the Arctic Interagency Visitor Center and Wiseman's cabins, plus a Bettles stop. Links: **plane hops** from Fairbanks/Bettles/Coldfoot to the pass, the gravel bars and the Alatna; **packraft floats** as the travel between river points.
- **Hubs & set pieces**: the Anaktuvuk Pass ranger station and the **Simon Paneak Memorial Museum** (Nunamiut exhibits — present them with the community's own words), the village's airstrip; the Coldfoot Arctic Interagency Visitor Center, Wiseman's 1908 log cabins; 3D landmarks: **Frigid Crags and Boreal Mountain** as a gateway pair, Mount Doonerak, the **Arrigetch spires**, Mount Igikpak; floatplanes and tundra-tyre planes landing on water and gravel; a packraft and a tent as the player's « house ».
- **Activities**: fly-in camping (choose a landing spot, set camp on durable ground, store food in bear canisters), off-trail navigation (map and compass; no trails!), packrafting and canoeing the wild rivers, caribou watching from a ridge (a migration you cannot hurry), photo spots — the Gates from the river, Arrigetch spires at midnight, caribou crossing the John River, Walker Lake, the Arctic Circle sign, aurora over Wiseman; Dall sheep spotting; fishing for grayling; learning Nunamiut and Iñupiaq names (Anaktuvuk = « place of caribou droppings », Arrigetch = « fingers of the outstretched hand »); Junior Ranger; winter mode with aurora, dog teams and snowshoes; a « leave no trace » scoring of your camp.
- **Passport stamps**: « Bettles Field » (Bettles Ranger Station), « Coldfoot » (Arctic Interagency Visitor Center), « Anaktuvuk Pass » (ranger station and Simon Paneak Memorial Museum) and « Fairbanks » (the park office / Alaska Public Lands Information Center).
- **With friends / with the world**: a party shares one small plane (weight limits) and a packraft flotilla; river crossings arm-in-arm; spreading out on the tundra so that no path forms (a co-op « no trace » mechanic); a shared midnight-sun summit or aurora-watching session; gathering spots — the Coldfoot visitor center, the Anaktuvuk Pass airstrip on plane days, a gravel-bar camp at the Gates.
- **Rules of the park** (Leave No Trace, as gameplay): no trails — spread out and walk on durable surfaces (gravel, rock), never in single file across tundra; camp on gravel bars or durable ground, move camp often; carry and use bear-resistant food containers; pack out all trash, bury human waste far from water; respect Native lands and subsistence camps (camp at least ½ mile away, one night per site on Corporation lands, ask permission otherwise); never disturb migrating caribou or block their path; cross rivers carefully; leave antlers, artifacts and rocks where they lie; tell someone your itinerary (help is days away).
- **Implementation challenges**: a world with no trails and no roads — navigation, pathfinding and the feel of tussock tundra; herds of hundreds of caribou moving as a flow; 24-hour sun and polar night lighting; aurora; braided rivers usable as landing strips and as float routes (packraft physics); bush-plane landing on water, gravel and tundra; aufeis and seasonal ice; very sparse, huge scenery that must still be fun at small scale.
